The extensional system of Semantics I

A model. M = ⟨D, I⟩. A domain D of individuals; an interpretation function I mapping each individual and predicate to its meaning (in the model); an assignment function g which maps variables to values. Evaluation is written ⟦α⟧M,g.

1

Substitution

Context. The Morning Star and the Evening Star in fact pick out the same object, namely Venus, but it is possible to be ignorant of this fact. ⟦the Morning Star⟧ = ⟦the Evening Star⟧.

  1. (1)The Morning Star is a planet.
  2. (2)The Evening Star is a planet.
  3. (3)Anna believes that the Morning Star is visible at dawn.
  4. (4)Anna believes that the Evening Star is visible at dawn.
Substitution
  1. Replace a term with one of the same extension.
  2. Check whether the truth value can change.

Exercise.

(a)Draw the composition tree for (1) in our extensional system. Treat the Morning Star as a name denoting m. Label every node with its denotation and its type.
(b)Do the same for (3). Give the denotation and the type of the embedded clause, then state the type believes must take to combine with it.
(c)m = e. Swap the leaf and recompute for (4). Find the node at which the derivation stops telling (3) and (4) apart.

Frege 1892. “Über Sinn und Bedeutung”.

2

Temperature

Context. Both sentences are uttered at the same moment, and both are true.

  1. (5)The temperature is 15 degrees.
  2. (6)The temperature is rising.

Given extensional substitution, why would these sentences be problematic?

Substitution into (6): #15 degrees is rising. False, though (5) and (6) are true.

Montague 1973. “The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English”. The puzzle is attributed there to Barbara Partee.

3

Existence

Context. Unicorns and jackalopes are fictional entities. Their extensions (sets) are empty. ⟦jackalope⟧ = ⟦unicorn⟧ = ∅.

  1. (7)Jackalopes are fierce.
  2. (8)Sue believes that jackalopes are fierce.
  3. (9)Sue believes that unicorns are fierce.
  4. (10)Emma is seeking a unicorn.
  5. (11)Emma found a unicorn.
Existential commitment
  1. Append and there is no such thing.
  2. Check whether the result contradicts.

Exercise.

(a)Run the test on (7), (8), (10), (11). Sort by whether the speaker is committed.
Committed: (7), (11). Not committed: (8), (10).
(b)(8) and (9) can differ in truth value. State precisely why the extensional system cannot let them.
The contribution of the noun is a set, in this case both the empty set, so the whole sentence must have the same truth value.
(c)Show that (10) and (11) come apart although both contain a unicorn.
(11) contains an existential presupposition about unicorns and (10) does not.

Frege 1892. “Über Sinn und Bedeutung”.

Russell 1905. “On Denoting”.

Strawson 1950. “On Referring”.

Montague 1970. “Universal Grammar”.

4

Specificity

Context. Ralph glimpses a man in a brown hat, at night, under circumstances that look like espionage. He takes that man to be a spy. Separately, Ralph has met a grey-haired man at the beach, a pillar of the local community, whom he knows by name as Bernard Ortcutt. He takes that man to be no spy. The man in the brown hat and the man at the beach are one person. Ralph does not know this. Put to him directly, Ralph will assent to that man is a spy of the first sighting, and deny Ortcutt is a spy.

  1. (12)Ralph believes that the man in the brown hat is a spy.
  2. (13)Ralph believes that the man at the beach is not a spy.
  3. (14)Ralph believes that Ortcutt is a spy.
  4. (15)Ralph believes that someone is a spy.
  5. (16)Emma wants to marry a Norwegian.
Continuation
  1. Append namely, Ortcutt, then but no one in particular.
  2. Note which continuation the sentence accepts.

Exercise.

(a)Judge (12) to (14) in the scenario.
(12) true. (13) true. (14) false de dicto; true de re.
(b)Run the continuation test on (15) and (16). Give each its two readings.
Both accept both continuations. (15): ∃x[believe(r, spy(x))]; believe(r, ∃x spy(x)). (16): ∃x[norwegian(x) ∧ want(emma, marry(emma, x))]; want(emma, ∃x[norwegian(x) ∧ marry(emma, x)]).
(c)Label the readings de re and de dicto from their behaviour on the test.
Namely, Ortcutt: de re. But no one in particular: de dicto.

Quine 1956. “Quantifiers and propositional attitudes”, the source of Ralph, Ortcutt and the brown hat. The labels are medieval: Abelard, twelfth century, separating a claim about a dictum from one about a res. Russell (1905) has a treatment of George IV, Scott, and the Author of Waverley.

5

Modal displacement

Context. One World Trade Center is in fact the tallest building in New York, but it is possible to be ignorant of this fact. Separately: the ground is wet this morning, and no one observed the night.

  1. (17)The Empire State Building might be the tallest building in New York.
  2. (18)The Empire State Building might be One World Trade Center.
  3. (19)It must have rained overnight.
  4. (20)It rained overnight.
Substitution, again
  1. The procedure from block 1, unchanged.
  2. Now applied inside might.

Exercise.

(a)Apply the test to (17) and (18). State the result.
(17) true, (18) false. ‘Might’ creates an opaque environment.
(b)Describe a situation in which (19) is warranted and (20) false, and one in which (20) is true and (19) unwarranted.
Warranted, false: wet from the street cleaners. True, unwarranted: you watched it rain.
(c)Say what fixes the truth of (19), given that the facts of the actual world do not.
The evidence. True iff it rained in every world compatible with the evidence.

Carnap 1947; Kripke 1963. C. I. Lewis built the first modal systems (1918, 1932) against the paradoxes of material implication. Carnap’s Meaning and necessity recast Leibniz’s worlds as state-descriptions and defined intension as a function to extension. Kripke added accessibility. Karttunen (1972) on must as marking inference.

6

Conditionals

Context. The moon is not made of cheese. Dublin is in Ireland. Anna will pass whatever she does. Oswald shot Kennedy, and no one else did.

  1. (21)If the moon is made of cheese, Dublin is in Ireland.
  2. (22)If Anna revises, she will pass.
  3. (23)If you strike this match, it will light.
  4. (24)If you strike this match in a vacuum, it will light.
  5. (25)If Oswald did not shoot Kennedy, someone else did.
  6. (26)If Oswald had not shot Kennedy, someone else would have.

Recall. p → q is false only when p is true and q false. True in every other case.

Truth table
  1. Read off the value p → q assigns, given the actual values of p and q.
  2. Check whether speakers accept the conditional.

Exercise.

(a)Give the value the material conditional assigns to (21) and (22). Say in each case what forces it, and whether you accept the sentence.
(21) true (false antecedent); odd/unacceptable. (22) true (true consequent); acceptable.
(b)(23) is true. Show that the material conditional makes (24) follow from it. Say whether it does follow.
p → q ⊨ (p ∧ r) → q. Does not follow: (24) false.
(c)(25) and (26) share an antecedent and a consequent. Show that the material conditional gives them one value, then say what speakers judge.
Both true (false antecedent). Generally (25) true, (26) false.
