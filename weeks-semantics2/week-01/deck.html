The question behind the term

Frege's Puzzle

The facts
The Morning Star and the Evening Star pick out one object. If meaning were reference, (1) and (2) would feel exactly alike.
The asymmetry
They don't. \(a=a\) is trivial; \(a=b\) can be news. Meaning tracks the mode of presentation as well as the referent.
Last year
Semantics I computes what expressions pick out in one world: extensions. A beautiful machine, and it cannot see this difference.
This term
Semantics II rebuilds meaning across possible worlds. Extension gives way to intension: negation, modality, attitudes, conditionals, aspect, evidentiality, all the operators above the verb.

Act I · The system we built

The model

The model
\(M = \langle D, I\rangle\): a domain of individuals and an interpretation. Constants to members of \(D\); one-place predicates to subsets; two-place predicates to relations on \(D \times D\).
Assignments
\(g\) values the variables: \(\sem{u}^{M,g} = g(u)\). Everything else is fixed by \(I\).
The point
Denotation is always denotation in a model: a stand-in for the facts, one complete way for things to be. Hold that thought for Act II.
Today's toy model
\[ D=\{n, a, d\} \qquad I(\textit{single})=\{n,a\} \qquad I(\textit{tall})=\{a,d\} \qquad I(\textit{admires})=\{\langle n,a\rangle, \langle a,d\rangle\} \]
Neil, Aoife and Dara: our domain for the hour
Act I · The system we built

Truth in the model

At the bottom
Predication is membership: a sentence is true when the named things stand in the named set or relation.
Going up
The connectives see only the parts' truth values. Nothing else about the parts survives. Remember that; the trouble starts exactly here.
The recursive clauses
\[ \sem{\pi(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)}^{M,g} = T \;\text{ iff }\; \langle\sem{\alpha_1}^{M,g},\dots,\sem{\alpha_n}^{M,g}\rangle \in \sem{\pi}^{M,g} \]
\[ \sem{\neg\varphi} = T \text{ iff } \sem{\varphi} = F \qquad \sem{\varphi \wedge \psi} = T \text{ iff } \sem{\varphi} = T \text{ and } \sem{\psi} = T \]
in the toy model: ⟦Neil is single⟧ = T, since n ∈ I(single)
Act I · The system we built

Quantifiers and assignments

The trick
An assignment variant \(g[u \mapsto k]\) is \(g\) with the variable \(u\) re-pointed at \(k\). Quantifiers survey the variants.
No names needed
A quantified sentence names nobody. It claims that some (or every) way of valuing the variable verifies the scope. Everything still bottoms out in membership in \(D\).
The two clauses
\[ \sem{\exists u.\varphi}^{M,g} = T \;\text{ iff for some } k \in D:\; \sem{\varphi}^{M,g[u\mapsto k]} = T \]
\[ \sem{\forall u.\varphi}^{M,g} = T \;\text{ iff for all } k \in D:\; \sem{\varphi}^{M,g[u\mapsto k]} = T \]
in the toy model: ∃x(single(x) ∧ tall(x)), verified by g[x ↦ a]
Act I · The system we built

Types, λ and Function Application

Types
Two basic types, \(e\) and \(t\), and functions \(\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle\) between them: predicates \(\langle e,t\rangle\), transitives \(\langle e,\langle e,t\rangle\rangle\), a sentence \(t\).
One rule
Composition is Function Application. The types say which sister is the function; λ says what it does with its argument.
The core schema
\[ \sem{\beta\;\gamma} = \sem{\beta}\left(\sem{\gamma}\right) \qquad\qquad \sem{\textit{admires}} = \lambda x.\lambda y.\,\textit{admires}(y,x) \]
from lexicon to truth condition, mechanically: compositionality at work
Act I · The system we built

Events and times

Davidson
Verbs describe events; sentences quantify over them; adverbs are predicates of the event variable. Modification is conjunction.
Tense
Times came last: tense locates the event relative to now. Events and times enlarged the ontology. Every claim was still checked against one world's facts.
The Davidsonian move
(3)Neil giggled loudly.⇝ \(\exists e\,(\textit{giggle}(e,n) \wedge \textit{loud}(e) \wedge e \prec \textit{now})\); drop a conjunct and Neil giggled follows for free
Act I · The system we built

Worked: Neil admires Aoife

Lexicon
\(\sem{\textit{Neil}} = n \qquad \sem{\textit{Aoife}} = a \qquad \sem{\textit{admires}} = \lambda x.\lambda y.\,\textit{admires}(y,x)\)
Types
\(e \qquad e \qquad \langle e,\langle e,t\rangle\rangle\)
VP, by Function Application
\(\sem{\textit{admires Aoife}} = \lambda y.\,\textit{admires}(y,a)\), type \(\langle e,t\rangle\)
S, by Function Application
\(\sem{\textit{Neil admires Aoife}} = \textit{admires}(n,a)\), type \(t\)
Evaluate in \(M\)
\(T\) iff \(\langle n,a\rangle \in I(\textit{admires})\): true in our toy model
Act I closes
Every value ⟦·⟧ handed us was a fact about one world: an individual, a set, a truth value.Extensions, all the way down. Now watch what ordinary English does to them.

Where extensional meaning runs out

Substitution failures, modal and attitude contexts, non-referential readings: the data a truth-in-a-model theory cannot capture.
Empirical

Leibniz's Law

Swap co-referring terms
(4)The Morning Star is a planet.true
(5)The Evening Star is a planet.true; the same verdict is guaranteed, since the two terms co-refer
The law
Substituting co-referring expressions preserves truth: salva veritate. Environments where this holds are extensional, or transparent.
Act I's promise
A theorem of the Semantics I system. If all ⟦·⟧ tracks is extension, sentences differing in co-extensional parts cannot differ in truth value.
Anna failed astronomy. Run the same swap
(6)
Anna believes that the Morning Star is visible at dawn.
(7)
Anna believes that the Evening Star is visible at dawn.
Empirical

Opacity

(6), in Anna's scenario(7), after the swap
True
She watches it every morning and says so.
Can be false
“Evening Star” means nothing to her; she would deny it outright.
Modals misbehave the same way
(8)The Empire State Building might be the tallest building in New York.a sensible thing to wonder
(9)#The Empire State Building might be One World Trade Center.absurd, although the two descriptions co-refer if (8)'s guess is right
(10)Little Emma thinks her house is the tallest building in New York.⇏ Little Emma thinks her house is One World Trade Center
The diagnostic
Same extensions, different truth values: these environments are opaque. Substitutivity is the field's first test for intensionality. Run it on anything.
Empirical

The opaque environments

Attitude verbsalternatives: someone's beliefs
Harry is single: checkable against the facts. Sue thinks that Harry is single: true or false whatever Harry's love life.
Modalsalternatives: rules, facts, goals, evidence
Every car is registered vs every car must be registered; the second can be true while the first is false. The form stays constant; the flavour (rules, evidence, goals) and the force (must vs might) vary. Weeks 3 and 4.
Epistemic adverbsalternatives: what is known
Possibly, Hannah is in the library. Where Hannah actually is does not settle it.
Intensional transitivesno actual-world witness needed
Emma is seeking a unicorn: true this morning, unicorns or no unicorns.
One family
Each construction drags in alternatives to the actual facts: beliefs, obligations, knowledge states, goals. That word is about to become a technical term. Conditionals join the family in Week 8; the progressive in Week 10.
True this morning. There are no unicorns
Emma is seeking a unicorn.
Empirical

Empty predicates

Who is committed to what
(11)Jackalopes are fierce.commits me to jackalopes
(12)Sue believes that jackalopes are fierce.commits Sue; I am off the hook
(13)Emma is seeking a unicorn.commits nobody to unicorns
The shift
Embedding moves existential commitment from the speaker to the attitude-holder. Referents inside the complement answer to the attitude.
The killer fact
Extensionally, \(\sem{\textit{jackalope}} = \emptyset = \sem{\textit{unicorn}}\). Act I literally cannot distinguish believing in jackalopes from believing in unicorns.
Empirical

The nesting problem

What Act I gives the complementWhat would follow
A truth value
\(\sem{\textit{Harry is single}} = T\). The clause hands thinks the value T, and nothing else.
Disaster
Any true complement becomes interchangeable; believing one truth means believing them all.
The absurd inference
(14)Sue thinks that Harry is single.true, say
(15)#Sue thinks that Dublin is in Ireland.should follow on the type-\(t\) analysis; both complements denote T
Upshot
The meaning of an embedded clause is richer than its truth value, so \(\langle t,\langle e,t\rangle\rangle\) for thinks is hopeless. The Formal lens repairs this today.
Empirical

De re and de dictoWeek 5

Ralph sees a man in a trench coat in an alley: that man is a spy. He sees a man on the beach: no spy, he. Both are Ortcutt. Quine (1956), “Quantifiers and propositional attitudes”
Ortcutt under two descriptions
(16)Ralph believes that the man in the alley is a spy.true; Ralph assents on the spot
(17)Ralph believes that Ortcutt is a spy.true de re (of the man himself) · false de dicto (under Ralph's descriptions)
The contrast
De dicto: the believer's mode of presentation matters. De re: the ascription targets the individual, however described. Abelard saw this in the 12th century. The handout works the paraphrases.

Historical

Aristotle's sea battle

There will be a sea battle tomorrow. De Interpretatione 9
Aristotle
384 – 322 BCE · after Lysippos · public domain
De Interpretatione 9: the argument that made the future a problem for logic.
The bind
True now, and the battle seems fated; false now, and it is fated to fail. Either way the future closes. Bivalence collides with an open future.
The Stoics
A propositional logic with a modal sorting: possible, necessary, impossible, contingent. Diodorus' Master Argument asks whether possibility collapses into actuality.
The point
Modality has meant alternatives to how things actually are from the very beginning. Act II's data are the oldest problem in logic.
Historical

The medievals and Leibniz

The schoolmen
Iohannes Buridanus · Kraków, BJ cod. 1771
Modal syllogistics at its technical peak: de dicto vs de re read directly off scope.
G. W. Leibniz
1646 – 1716 · Francke, c. 1695 · public domain
Possible worlds as complete alternatives, one actualised; necessity as truth in all of them. Week 3's vocabulary, assembled.
Abelard, 12th c.
First clear articulation of the de dicto / de re contrast in modal statements: the distinction Ralph and Ortcutt just needed.
Scotus, 13th–14th c.
Possibility as non-contradiction; contingency as could have been otherwise at the very moment of occurrence. Genuine alternatives, synchronically.
Historical

Sinn und Bedeutung

Gottlob Frege
1848 – 1925
“Über Sinn und Bedeutung” (1892), written to solve exactly our sentences (1) and (2).
The split
Every expression has a Bedeutung (its reference) and a Sinn: the mode of presentation of the referent. One Venus; two ways of being given Venus.
The payoff
\(a=a\) trivial, \(a=b\) news: the senses differ. In attitude reports expressions refer indirectly, to their customary sense. That is why (6) and (7) come apart.
The gap
A diagnosis awaiting a theory: Frege never says what senses are, mathematically. The gap stands open for fifty-five years.
Historical

Carnap and Quine

Rudolf Carnap
1891 – 1970
Meaning and Necessity (1947): senses become mathematical objects at last.
W. V. O. Quine
1908 – 2000 · photo 1980 · CC BY 4.0
“Quantifiers and propositional attitudes” (1956): opacity's sternest examiner.
The construction
State-descriptions: complete alternative ways things could be. The intension of an expression is a function from state-descriptions to extensions; necessity is truth in all of them. Leibniz, formalised.
The resistance
Opacity infects quantification as well as substitution: which \(x\) is such that Ralph believes \(x\) is a spy? Quine demanded an explicit semantics before conceding an inch.
Still missing
Carnap built intensional syntax without a semantics for the modal operators themselves. The missing piece is accessibility between worlds: Kripke (1963), and Week 3.
Historical

Kripke, Hintikka, Montague

Jaakko Hintikka
1929 – 2015 · via Wikimedia Commons
Knowledge and Belief (1962): attitudes as quantification over an agent's epistemic alternatives. Week 5.
Saul Kripke
1940 – 2022 · UCSB lecture · CC BY 2.0
“Semantical considerations” (1963): worlds plus accessibility; ◻ and ◇ as quantifiers at last. Week 3.
RM
Richard Montague
1930 – 1971 · no free photograph exists
“English as a formal language”: intensional types, modals, attitudes, scope, one compositional fragment. Home ground.
Prior, 1957
Tense logic runs alongside: temporal operators quantify over times exactly as modals will over worlds. One template for both. Weeks 10 and 12.
This module
Semantics I built the extensional half of Montague's fragment. This term builds the other half: Kripke's worlds in Week 3, Kratzer on their shoulders in Week 4, Hintikka's attitudes in Week 5.

From models to intensions

The core new idea: an intension as a function from possible worlds (and times) to extensions, and what it immediately buys.
Formal

Extension and intension

Carnap's functions, promoted to the meaning itself. The model quietly grows a set \(W\) of worlds.
Extension
What an expression picks out at the world of evaluation: an individual, a set, a truth value. Everything Act I ever computed.
Intension
A function from worlds to extensions: \(\lambda w.\sem{\alpha}^{w}\). The Taoiseach: extension, the current officeholder; intension, whoever heads the government at each \(w\). Two descriptions can share an extension while their intensions differ. Exactly what (6) to (10) demanded. Times enter as a second index; the Week 12 capstone returns to them.
The intensional type inventory
\[ \textit{names}:\ \langle s,e\rangle \qquad \textit{predicates}:\ \langle s,\langle e,t\rangle\rangle \qquad \textit{sentences}:\ \langle s,t\rangle \]
the answer to “what could a Sinn be, mathematically?”, and the inventory the Typological lens interrogates
Formal

Propositions as sets of worlds

The proposition a sentence expresses
\[ \sem{S} \;=\; \{\,w : S \text{ is true at } w\,\}, \quad\text{type } \langle s,t\rangle; \qquad S \text{ is true at } w \text{ iff } w \in \sem{S} \]
(18)Carlingford is in Northern Ireland.perfectly intelligible while its truth value is unknown to you; meaning is the condition. (Carlingford is in Louth.)
The old toolkit lifts
Entailment is subset, negation is complement, conjunction is intersection: the set theory of Semantics I, run on worlds. Week 2 puts entailment straight to work: downward-entailing environments and NPI licensing.
Why it matters
A sentence meaning is now an object rich enough to be embedded. The nesting problem can be repaired.
Formal

Re-typing believe

The complement denotes a proposition
\(\sem{\textit{that Harry is single}} = \{\,w : \textit{Harry is single at } w\,\}\), type \(\langle s,t\rangle\)
Re-type the verb
\(\sem{\textit{thinks}} : \langle\langle s,t\rangle,\langle e,t\rangle\rangle\); it takes a proposition
Function Application, as ever
\(\sem{\textit{thinks that Harry is single}} = \sem{\textit{thinks}}\left(\sem{\textit{that Harry is single}}\right)\)
The bad inference dies
\(\{w : \textit{Harry single at } w\} \neq \{w : \textit{Dublin in Ireland at } w\}\); (14) no longer drags in (15)
One question remains
\(\textit{Still open: the relation thinks names between Sue and that set of worlds.}\)
Formal

HintikkaWeek 5

An attribution of any propositional attitude involves a division of all the possible worlds into two classes: those in accordance with the attitude, and those incompatible with it. Hintikka, Knowledge and Belief (1962), lightly trimmed
The move
\[ \mathrm{DOX}(x) = \{\,w : w \text{ is compatible with everything } x \text{ believes}\,\} \qquad \sem{\textit{believes}} = \lambda p_{\langle s,t\rangle}.\lambda x_e.\; \mathrm{DOX}(x) \subseteq p \]
(19)Sue believes that it is raining in Dublin.true iff it rains in Dublin throughout DOX(Sue); belief as truth throughout one's belief-worlds
What it buys
Frege's puzzle: distinct propositions, distinct arguments. Empty predicates: Sue's belief-worlds may contain jackalopes. The debts (where \(\mathrm{DOX}\) comes from, logical omniscience) fall due in Weeks 3 to 5.

LENS 04 / 04 · LIU33008 · Week 1

Is the type inventory universal?

Whether every language encodes the same intensional distinctions: the cross-linguistic thread the term keeps returning to.
Typological

Three signposts

If worlds and \(\langle s,t\rangle\) are the universal space, languages still differ in which corners grammar must mention.
Variable force
  • St'át'imcets modals fix flavour lexically; force stays open
  • k'a epistemic, ka deontic; must-or-might from context
  • the mirror image of English
Rullmann, Matthewson & Davis 2008 → Week 4
Shifted indexicals
  • Amharic I under say can be the reported speaker
  • “John says that I am a hero” = John's I
  • attitudes can shift the context itself
Schlenker 2003 → Week 5
Evidential source
  • Cuzco Quechua marks how you know: =mi direct, =si reportative, =chá conjectural
  • epistemic modal, or speech-act operator
Faller 2002 → Week 11
The question
Same space of worlds with different grammaticalised distinctions, or genuinely different inventories? Every typological section this term is one round of that argument.
