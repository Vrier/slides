Must and might

Modal words as quantifiers over possibilities.
A five-letter word game. Decide: true or false
(1)
It must end in E.
(2)
It might start with C.
(3)
It must contain a T.
(4)
It might be CRIME.
Five candidates left: CRANE · CRATE · GRAPE · TRACE · BRACE
Empirical

Must and might

mustmight
All candidates
(1) true: every candidate ends in E. (3) false: GRAPE has no T.
Some candidate
(2) true: CRANE, CRATE. (4) false: CRIME is off the list.
The domain
The candidates: the possibilities compatible with what is known.
Worlds
A possibility that settles every question: a possible world.
Empirical

Possible worlds

A world
One complete way things could be. Every sentence settled, true or false.
A proposition
The set of worlds where it is true: a region of the space.
Empirical

Duality

Every and some
(5)Not every student passed.= Some student did not pass. \(\;\neg\forall \equiv \exists\neg\)
(6)No student passed.= Every student failed to pass. \(\;\neg\exists \equiv \forall\neg\)
Must and may
(7)You needn't come.= You may stay away. \(\;\neg\Box \equiv \Diamond\neg\)
(8)You mustn't smoke.= Smoking is not permitted. \(\;\Box\neg \equiv \neg\Diamond\)
Notation
\(\Box\): necessity, all. \(\Diamond\): possibility, some. \(\;\Box p \equiv \neg\Diamond\neg p\).
Decide: what the negation applies to
(9)
You may not leave.
(10)
She may not be home.
The same string: may not.
Empirical

Scope

(9), about the rules(10), about the evidence
\(\neg\Diamond\)
It is not permitted that you leave.
\(\Diamond\neg\)
It is possible that she is not home.
Against the surface order
(11)I can't seem to find my keys.seem over not over can
The pattern
Modals take scope relative to negation and to each other, as every and some do.
Empirical

Flavours

Epistemicin view of what is known
The lights are on. Anna must be home.
Deonticin view of the rules
Visitors must sign in.
Circumstantialin view of the circumstances
I must sneeze.
Teleologicalin view of a goal
To reach Howth, you must take the DART.
GapsPortner 2009
It may be raining. #It can be raining. It can't be raining. Epistemic can needs negation.
Constant and variable
The force stays: all. The domain varies with the in view of phrase. Kratzer (1977).
Decide: does the plain sentence follow
(12)
Sue knows that it is raining.
(13)
Visitors must sign in.
(14)
Anna must be home.
It is raining · Visitors sign in · Anna is home
Empirical

Flavours and inference

Knowledge(12): follows
#Sue knows that it is raining, but it isn't.
Obligation(13): does not follow
Visitors must sign in, and some never do.
Epistemic must(14): disputed
Fine on seeing wet umbrellas. Odd while watching the rain fall. Karttunen (1972); von Fintel & Gillies (2010).
Upshot
The same quantifier over different domains licenses different inferences.

Historical

C. I. Lewis

The material conditional
\(p \rightarrow q\) is false in one case only: \(p\) true, \(q\) false. True whenever \(p\) is false. True whenever \(q\) is true.
Both true
(15)If the moon is made of cheese, then 2 + 2 = 5.false antecedent
(16)If it is raining in Dublin, then 2 + 2 = 4.true consequent
Strict implication
Lewis (1912, 1918): implication as a necessary connection. \(p\) strictly implies \(q\): \(\;\Box(p \rightarrow q)\).
The gap
Lewis & Langford (1932): five axiom systems, S1 to S5, and no semantics. No way to settle whether \(\Box p \rightarrow \Box\Box p\) holds.
Historical

Carnap: state-descriptions

Rudolf Carnap
1891 – 1970
Meaning and Necessity (1947), §2 and §39.
State-description
A class of sentences: for every atomic sentence, either it or its negation. A complete description of a possible state of the universe.
His gloss
State-descriptions stand for Leibniz's possible worlds.
What it supplies
A detailed semantics for a modal operator. Lewis's systems had axioms only.
Historical

Carnap: necessity

Necessity
\(N(S)\) is true iff \(S\) holds in every state-description.
No relation
Every state-description counts, from every state-description. One logic results: S5.

Historical · What a world is

Lewis's realism

David Lewis
1941 – 2001 · photo 1962 · public domain
Counterfactuals (1973); On the Plurality of Worlds (1986).
Two questions
What is a possible world. What is it for something to exist in one. Menzel (SEP).
The argument
Things could have been otherwise in many ways. Ways things could have been exist. Call them worlds.
What worlds are
Concrete, of a kind with ours: a maximal spatiotemporally connected object. To exist in one is to be part of it. Actual works like here: an indexical.
The reply
The incredulous stare. His answer: worlds earn their place by use, as sets do in mathematics.
Counterparts
Worlds do not overlap, so nothing exists in two of them and nothing in one causes anything in another. What Anna might have done is what her counterparts do. I dreamt that I was Brigitte Bardot and that I kissed me (Lakoff).
Historical · What a world is

Three answers

ConcretismD. Lewis
A world is a concrete universe. To exist in it is to be a part of it.
AbstractionismAdams · Plantinga · Stalnaker
A world is an abstract total way things could be: a set of propositions holding \(p\) or its negation for every \(p\) (Adams), or a maximal possible state of affairs (Plantinga). To exist in it is for it to include your existing.
CombinatorialismArmstrong · the Tractatus
A world is a recombination of the actual world's simples. Carnap's state-descriptions are the version built from sentences.
The cost of the second
Possible and consistent are themselves modal, so the worlds are defined in the vocabulary they were meant to explain. The extensional semantics is lost.
Kripke's advice
Worlds are stipulated. Two dice: thirty-six states, thirty-six small worlds. Grain as fine as the question needs; the word game needed five.
Historical · What a world is

Fictionalism

Rosen (1990)
Talk of worlds as talk within a fiction, on a par with Holmes at 221B Baker Street. \(\Diamond p\) iff, according to the fiction of possible worlds, \(p\) holds at some world.
According to the fiction
(17)In the Holmes stories, a detective lives at 221B Baker Street.true
(18)In the Holmes stories, Holmes has blood type O.neither true nor false: the stories leave it open
The operator
According to the fiction is an intensional operator. Lewis (1978) analyses it with possible worlds.
Also available to the third
Armstrong declines merely possible states of affairs any standing, and speaks as if they exist.
A working position
Worlds as points in a model. Each settles every sentence; a relation holds among them.

Frames, models, validity

\(\Box\) and \(\Diamond\) as quantifiers over accessible worlds.
Formal

Necessity

\(\Box p\) at \(w_0\)
Every world \(w_0\) sees lies inside \(p\). (13) Visitors must sign in: true.
And \(w_0\) itself
Outside \(p\). Some visitors never sign in. \(\Box p\) true, \(p\) false.
Formal

Possibility

\(\Diamond q\) at \(w_0\)
Some world \(w_0\) sees lies inside \(q\): \(w_1\). Visitors may use the lift: true.
Duality, pictured
\(w_2\) and \(w_3\) lie outside \(q\), so \(\Box q\) fails at \(w_0\). \(\neg\Box q \equiv \Diamond\neg q\).
Formal

Frames and models

Language
Propositional logic, plus \(\Box\varphi\) and \(\Diamond\varphi\).
Frame
\(\langle W, R\rangle\): a set of worlds and an accessibility relation \(R \subseteq W \times W\). \(wRw'\): \(w\) sees \(w'\).
Model
A frame plus a valuation: \(V(p)\), the set of worlds where \(p\) is true.
A model
Last week's four worlds. \(p\): it is raining in Dublin. \(q\): Harry is single. Arrows: \(R\).
Formal

Truth at a world

The two clauses
\[ M,w \models \Box\varphi \;\text{ iff for every } w' \text{ such that } wRw':\; M,w' \models \varphi \] \[ M,w \models \Diamond\varphi \;\text{ iff for some } w' \text{ such that } wRw':\; M,w' \models \varphi \]
the connectives keep their usual clauses, checked at \(w\)
As sets
\(R(w)\): the worlds \(w\) sees. \(\Box\varphi\) at \(w\): \(R(w) \subseteq \sem{\varphi}\). \(\Diamond\varphi\) at \(w\): \(R(w) \cap \sem{\varphi} \neq \emptyset\).
Last week
Sue believes p iff \(\mathrm{DOX} \subseteq p\). The same clause, with belief as the relation.
Formal

Worked: the model at \(w_1\)

What \(w_1\) sees
\(R(w_1) = \{w_1, w_2\}\)
\(\Box p\) at \(w_1\)
\(\{w_1, w_2\} \subseteq V(p)\): true
\(\Diamond q\) at \(w_1\)
\(\{w_1, w_2\} \cap V(q) = \{w_1\}\): true
\(\Box q\) at \(w_1\)
\(w_2 \notin V(q)\): false
A dead end: \(w_4\)
\(R(w_4) = \emptyset\): \(\Box p\) true, \(\Diamond p\) false

\(\Box q\) at \(w_2\).
b.
\(q\) at \(w_2\).

\(\Diamond p\) at \(w_3\).

Formal

Portner's ants

SmellSight
Reflexive
In the nest every ant smells the ants around it, itself included.
Symmetric
Compound eyes: if ant 1 sees ant 2, ant 2 sees ant 1. No ant sees its own back.
What every ant can be sure of
(19)If every ant I smell is female, then I am female.holds for smell, whatever the facts: the shape of \(\Box p \rightarrow p\)
(20)If I have a white spot, then every ant I see sees an ant with a white spot.holds for sight: the shape of \(p \rightarrow \Box\Diamond p\)
The point
\(W\) can hold anything. What is valid follows from the shape of \(R\). Portner (2009: 11–13).
Formal

Validity

In a model
True at every world of the model.
On a frame
Valid in every model on the frame. Change the \(p\)s and \(q\)s; keep the arrows.
A countermodel
\[ \Box q \rightarrow q \quad \text{at } w_2: \qquad \Box q \text{ true}, \quad q \text{ false} \]
our model: \(R(w_2) = \{w_3\}\); \(q\) holds at \(w_3\) and fails at \(w_2\)
Countermodel
A model and a world where the formula is false. One is enough: the formula is not valid on that frame.
Formal

Frame conditions

T, also M\(\Box p \rightarrow p\)
Reflexive: every world sees itself.
D\(\Box p \rightarrow \Diamond p\)
Serial: every world sees some world.
4\(\Box p \rightarrow \Box\Box p\)
Transitive: whatever a seen world sees is seen.
B\(p \rightarrow \Box\Diamond p\)
Symmetric: seeing is mutual.
5\(\Diamond p \rightarrow \Box\Diamond p\)
Euclidean: worlds seen from one world see each other.
Systems
K: no condition. KD: D. S4: T + 4. S5: T + 5, with \(R\) an equivalence relation. KD45: D + 4 + 5.
Formal

Axioms and flavours

KnowledgeT · idealised: S5
\(wRw'\) iff everything \(i\) knows in \(w\) is true in \(w'\). Reflexive. #Sue knows that it is raining, but it isn't.
BeliefKD45
The same, with believes. Serial; reflexivity fails. Sue believes that it is raining, but it isn't.
ObligationKD
\(wRw'\) iff all the rules of \(w\) are followed in \(w'\). Serial; reflexivity fails. If Mary must tell the truth, then she will: no. …then she may: yes.
Future timetransitive, serial
\(\Box\) as always: (4) holds. Earlier and later are never mutual: (B) fails.
The move
A flavour is a choice of \(R\). Its logic follows from the properties of \(R\). Portner (2009: 20–23).
Formal

Must and may

Lexical entries
\[ \sem{\textit{must}}^{w} = \lambda R.\,\lambda p.\;\forall w'\,[\,R(w)(w') \rightarrow p(w')\,] \] \[ \sem{\textit{may}}^{w} = \lambda R.\,\lambda p.\;\exists w'\,[\,R(w)(w') \wedge p(w')\,] \]
\(R\): \(\langle s,\langle s,t\rangle\rangle\) · \(p\): \(\langle s,t\rangle\) · after von Fintel & Heim, chapter 2
In words
Must takes a relation from the context and a proposition. True at \(w\) iff the proposition holds at every world accessible from \(w\).
With world variables
\(\Box p\) spelled out in predicate logic: \(\forall w[\textit{acc}(\dot{w})(w) \rightarrow p(w)]\). Coppock & Champollion.
Division of labour
Force in the lexicon. Flavour from the context, as \(R\).
Formal

Substitution under necessity

Last week's test, run on necessarily
(21)Eight is necessarily greater than seven.true
(22)#The number of planets is necessarily greater than seven.false, although the number of planets is eight · after Quine (1953)
(23)Necessarily, all John's dogs are mammals.true; swap the coextensional John's pets and it turns false · Menzel (SEP)
The diagnostic
Co-referring terms, different truth values: necessarily creates an opaque environment.
With worlds
The number of planets picks out different numbers at different worlds. Eight picks out the same number at every world: a rigid designator. Kripke (1980).
Formal

Limits of the simple system Week 3

Whose knowledge, and whenPortner 2009
John says Mary must be lost: his knowledge, at the time of speaking. The theory equally allows Joan's, or last Friday's. \(R\) has to come from the context.
Must and shouldweak necessity
Most accessible worlds: both sets are infinite, so no ratio. A patch: should counts more rules, so it sees fewer worlds, and must p entails should p.
Boxes and diamondsCresswell; Coppock & Champollion
It might have been that everyone rich was poor. The sensible reading keeps rich at the actual world inside the scope of \(\Diamond\). No arrangement of boxes and diamonds says that.
Conflicting rulesseriality
A Catch-22: no world keeps every rule. \(R(w)\) is empty: every must true, every may false.

Nez Perce: a modal without a dual

-o'qa
One modal suffix. Translated can, could, may, should, must.

A possibility modal. No necessity modal competes with it, so no scalar implicature. It appears where English uses must.
