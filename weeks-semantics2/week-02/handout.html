The modal language

Definitions
Atomic propositionsp, q, r, …
Formulasφ ::= p | ¬φ | (φ ∧ ψ) | (φ ∨ ψ) | (φ → ψ) | □φ | ◇φ
□ANecessarily / must / always / knows that A.
◇APossibly / might / sometimes / it is compatible that A.
Material conditional(φ → ψ) is false when φ is true and ψ is false, and true otherwise.
DualityThe two modal operators are interdefinable: ◇A ≡ ¬□¬A and □A ≡ ¬◇¬A.
Parallel with ∀ and ∃□ and ◇ behave like quantifiers over accessible worlds: ¬∀ ≡ ∃¬ and ¬∃ ≡ ∀¬.
  1. (1)You needn't come.
  2. (2)You mustn't smoke.
  3. (3)You may not leave.
  4. (4)She may not be home.

Exercise.

(a)Choose an atomic proposition for each of (1) to (4) and translate the sentence with □, ◇ and ¬. (3) and (4) contain the same string may not: state the scope of ¬ in each.
(b)Use duality to give an equivalent formula for each translation, and an English sentence that expresses it.

Kripke semantics

Definitions
Frame⟨W, R⟩ with a non-empty set W of worlds and an accessibility relation R ⊆ W × W.
Model⟨W, R, V⟩ where V is a valuation assigning each atomic proposition p a set V(p) ⊆ W.
M, w ⊨ □φiff ∀w′ ∈ W (wRw′ ⇒ M, w′ ⊨ φ)
M, w ⊨ ◇φiff ∃w′ ∈ W (wRw′ ∧ M, w′ ⊨ φ)
R(w)The set of worlds accessible from w. □φ at w: R(w) ⊆ V(φ). ◇φ at w: R(w) ∩ V(φ) ≠ ∅.
NotationPortner writes ⟦α⟧w,M = 1 for M, w ⊨ α.
Checking a modal formula
  1. To check M, w ⊨ □A: look at all R-accessible worlds from w and see whether A holds there.
  2. To check M, w ⊨ ◇A: look for some accessible world where A holds.

Validity

Definitions
Valid in a modelTrue at every world of the model.
Valid on a frameValid in every model on that frame: true at every world, under every valuation.
CountermodelA model and a world at which the formula is false.

The ant model. W = {ant 1, ant 2, ant 3}. R is smells: ant 1 smells itself, ant 2 and ant 3; ant 2 smells itself and ant 1; ant 3 smells itself. p: this ant is female. q: this ant has a white spot. V(p) = {ant 1, ant 2}. V(q) = {ant 1}.

  1. (5)If every ant I smell is female, then I am female.
  2. (6)If I have a white spot, then every ant I smell smells an ant with a white spot.

Exercise.

(a)Draw the ant model: nodes, arrows, and the atomic propositions true at each node.
(b)Translate (5) and (6) into the modal language. Evaluate each at every ant.
(c)Keep the arrows and change V. Try to make your formula for (5) false at some ant. Then make your formula for (6) true at every ant. State what each attempt shows about validity on the frame.

Axioms and flavours

Definitions
EpistemicMust / might relative to an information state; knowledge and belief.
DeonticMust / should / may relative to rules or norms: obligation and permission.
TemporalAlways / sometimes in the future or past.
  1. (7)If it must be raining, then it is raining.
  2. (8)If Mary must tell the truth, then she will tell the truth.
  3. (9)If it must be raining, then it might be raining.
  4. (10)If Mary must tell the truth, then she may tell the truth.
  5. (11)#Sue knows that it is raining, but it isn't.
  6. (12)Sue believes that it is raining, but it isn't.

Exercise.

(a)For each of (7) to (10), name the axiom whose form it has, give the flavour of its modals, and judge whether it has to be true.
(b)Fill in the table: ✓ where the axiom is plausible for that reading of □, ✗ where it is not. Be ready to give the English judgement behind each mark.
□ read as(M)(D)(4)(B)
x knows that
x believes that
it is obligatory that
always in the future
Standard choices
  1. (M): plausible for knowledge, which is factive. Implausible for belief and for deontic ought.
  2. (D): if A is obligatory, then A is permitted. Often assumed for belief. Temporal: each time has an accessible future.
  3. (4): temporal always; with (5), the introspection ideals for knowledge and belief. (B) fails for tense: earlier and later are never symmetric.
  4. S5: an idealised logic of knowledge. KD: a standard deontic baseline. KD45: a standard logic of belief.

Accessibility relations

Definitions
Epistemic RwRw′ iff everything individual i knows in w is true in w′.
Deontic RwRw′ iff all the rules in force in w are followed in w′.
Bouletic RwRw′ iff all of i's desires in w are satisfied in w′.
ForceNecessity modals (□): must, should, would. Possibility modals (◇): may, might, can, could.
  1. (13)Fish can breathe water. ability
  2. (14)To get to my house, you must take a ferry. goal
  3. (15)Dogs may stay in this hotel.
  4. (16)Dogs can stay in this hotel.
  5. (17)It may be raining.
  6. (18)#It can be raining.
  7. (19)It can't be raining.

Exercise.

(a)On the pattern of the definitions, write an accessibility relation for (13) and one for (14). Then state the truth conditions of (14) using yours.
(b)From (15) to (19), state which flavours may allows and which can allows, and what (19) adds.
(c)Suppose must uses the rules in a set M and should uses the rules in a larger set S, so M ⊆ S. Show that Rshould(w) ⊆ Rmust(w). Then show that must p entails should p.

Substitution under necessity

Definitions
SubstitutivitySubstituting co-referring expressions preserves truth in extensional (transparent) environments but can fail in intensional (opaque) ones.
Rigid designatorAn expression that picks out the same individual relative to every world.
Coextensional predicatesPredicates true of exactly the same things: ∀x(Dx ↔ Px).
  1. (23)All John's dogs are mammals. John's only pets are two dogs
  2. (24)All John's pets are mammals.
  3. (25)Necessarily, all John's dogs are mammals.
  4. (26)Ruth Bader Ginsburg is necessarily Ruth Bader Ginsburg.
  5. (27)Ruth Bader Ginsburg was necessarily the first person to serve on both the Harvard and the Columbia law reviews.
  6. (28)Miss America is necessarily Miss America.

Exercise.

(a)(23) and (24) have the same truth value, so swapping one for the other inside a larger sentence should preserve truth. Do the swap in (25) and judge the result. Say which classical principle fails.
(b)In the actual world, Ginsburg was the first person to serve on both reviews. Run the same test on (26) and (27), give the truth value of each, and state what the result shows about necessarily.
(c)Miss America picks out different people in different worlds. Say why (25) is true all the same, referring to the worlds that necessarily quantifies over.

What you need to know

After this week you can
  1. Translate English modal sentences into □, ◇ and ¬, and convert between them by duality.
  2. Evaluate □φ and ◇φ at a world of a given model, including nested formulas and dead ends.
  3. Tell validity in a model from validity on a frame, and build a countermodel.
  4. Match (D), (M), (4), (B) and (5) to the frame conditions, and name K, T, S4, S5, KD and KD45.
  5. Argue from English judgements which conditions the accessibility relation of a flavour has.
  6. Write an accessibility relation for a given flavour.
  7. Move between □ / ◇ and explicit quantification over worlds, and say what the second can express that the first cannot.
