1

The modal language

A set of atomic propositions p, q, r, …; formulas φ ::= p | ¬φ | (φ ∧ ψ) | (φ ∨ ψ) | (φ → ψ) | □φ | ◇φ.

Definitions
Intended readings□A ‘necessarily / must / always / knows that A’. ◇A ‘possibly / might / sometimes / it is compatible that A’.
DualityThe two modal operators are interdefinable: ◇A ≡ ¬□¬A and □A ≡ ¬◇¬A.
Parallel with ∀ and ∃On Kripke semantics, □ and ◇ behave like quantifiers over accessible worlds. □A is true at w iff A is true at all worlds accessible from w (like ∀); ◇A iff A is true at some accessible world (like ∃).
Scope¬◇A: negation above the modal. ◇¬A: negation below it. ¬◇A is ‘it is not possible that A’, and ◇¬A is ‘it is possible that not A’.

2

Frame conditions

Normal modal logics start from K and add axioms. They can also be characterised by constraints on the accessibility relation R.

Core correspondences
(D)□A → ◇A⇔ Serial: ∀w ∃u (wRu)
(T)□A → A⇔ Reflexive: ∀w (wRw)
(4)□A → □□A⇔ Transitive: wRv ∧ vRu ⇒ wRu
(B)A → □◇A⇔ Symmetric: wRv ⇒ vRw
(5)◇A → □◇A⇔ Euclidean: wRv ∧ wRu ⇒ vRu
Further correspondences
(CD)◇A → □A⇔ Functional: wRv ∧ wRu ⇒ v = u
(□T)□(□A → A)⇔ Shift-reflexive: wRv ⇒ vRv
(C4)□□A → □A⇔ Dense: wRv ⇒ ∃u(wRu ∧ uRv)
(C)◇□A → □◇A⇔ Convergent: wRv ∧ wRx ⇒ ∃u(vRu ∧ xRu)
Named systems that are useful to linguistics
KThe minimal normal modal logic; baseline principles for □ and ◇.
TAdds □A → A. Often called M in the readings.
S4Reflexive + transitive (M4 in the readings).
S5Equivalence frames: reflexive + symmetric + transitive. Often used as an idealised logic of knowledge. The strongest normal logic.
KDA standard deontic baseline: seriality, and importantly not T.
KD45A standard doxastic logic (belief): serial + transitive + Euclidean.

3

Flavours and their logics

Modal logic studies operators that behave like necessity and possibility. In linguistic applications the same formal operators are used, with different accessibility relations, to model:

Flavours
AlethicMetaphysical or logical necessity and possibility.
EpistemicMust / might relative to an information state; knowledge and belief.
DeonticMust / should / may relative to rules or norms: obligation and permission.
CircumstantialCan / have to relative to the facts at issue: abilities, dispositions.
Bouletic, teleologicalRelative to desires, goals, plans.
Temporal‘Always’ / ‘sometimes’ in the future or past.
Which axioms are plausible to model what?
  1. (T) □A → A
    • Plausible for knowledge, which is factive: if x knows A, then A.
    • Not plausible for belief: people can believe false things.
    • Not plausible for deontic ought: ‘you ought to A’ does not entail ‘A’.
  2. (D) □A → ◇A
    • Deontic readings: if A is obligatory, then A is permitted.
    • Often assumed for belief: at least one world is compatible with what is believed.
    • Temporal: if there is always a future, each time has at least one accessible future. Not necessarily for the past.
  3. (4) □A → □□A
    • Temporal ‘always’: if A holds at all future times, then at all future times it will still be true that A holds at all future times.
    • Epistemic and doxastic: positive introspection ideals (KA → KKA, BA → BBA).
  4. (5) ◇A → □◇A, equivalently ¬□A → □¬□A
    • Epistemic and doxastic: negative introspection ideals.
    • Alethic: metaphysical and logical modality.
  5. (B) A → □◇A
    • Indistinguishability-style epistemic accessibility.
    • Not for tense: ordinary earlier and later relations are not symmetric.
  1. (14)If it must be raining, then it is raining.
  2. (15)If Mary must tell the truth, then she will tell the truth.
  3. (16)If it must be raining, then it might be raining.
  4. (17)If Mary must tell the truth, then she may tell the truth.

Frames, models, truth at a world

Definitions
Frame⟨W, R⟩ with a non-empty set W of worlds and an accessibility relation R ⊆ W × W. R(w) = {w′ : wRw′}, the worlds w sees.
Model⟨W, R, V⟩ where V is a valuation assigning each atomic proposition p a set V(p) ⊆ W.
M, w ⊨ □φiff ∀w′ ∈ W (wRw′ ⇒ M, w′ ⊨ φ). As sets: R(w) ⊆ ⟦φ⟧.
M, w ⊨ ◇φiff ∃w′ ∈ W (wRw′ ∧ M, w′ ⊨ φ). As sets: R(w) ∩ ⟦φ⟧ ≠ ∅.
Intuitively
  1. To check M, w ⊨ □A: look at all R-accessible worlds from w and see whether A holds there.
  2. To check M, w ⊨ ◇A: look for some accessible world where A holds.

Model M. The model on the Frames and models slide. V(p) = {w1, w2}. V(q) = {w1, w3}. Arrows show R.

Worked: the model at w1
  1. R(w1) = {w1, w2}. ⟦p⟧ = {w1, w2}, so R(w1) ⊆ ⟦p⟧: □p is true at w1.
  2. ⟦q⟧ = {w1, w3}. R(w1) ∩ ⟦q⟧ = {w1} ≠ ∅: ◇q is true. w2 ∉ ⟦q⟧: □q is false.
  3. Nested: □◇p at w1 needs ◇p at w1 and w2. R(w2) = {w3} and p is false there: □◇p is false.
  4. Dead end: R(w4) = ∅. Every □φ is true at w4 (there is no world to check) and every ◇φ is false.

Validity

Definitions
Valid in a modelTrue at every world of the model.
Valid on a frameValid in every model on that frame: true at every world, under every valuation.
CountermodelA model and a world at which the formula is false.

The ant model. W = {ant 1, ant 2, ant 3}. R is smells: ant 1 smells itself, ant 2 and ant 3; ant 2 smells itself and ant 1; ant 3 smells itself. p: this ant is female. q: this ant has a white spot. V(p) = {ant 1, ant 2}. V(q) = {ant 1}.

  1. (21)If every ant I smell is female, then I am female.
  2. (22)If I have a white spot, then every ant I smell smells an ant with a white spot.
