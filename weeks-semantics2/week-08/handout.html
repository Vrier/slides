Counterfactuals and similarity

Definitions
CounterfactualA conditional whose antecedent is known or assumed to be false. If A were, C would be: A □→ C.
Similarity orderingw1 ≤w w2 iff w1 is at least as similar to w as w2 is. No world is as similar to w as w itself.
Closest worldsMinw(p): the p-worlds to which no p-world is more similar.
Limit assumptionMinw(p) ≠ ∅ whenever p is true at some world.
Would-counterfactual⟦A □→ C⟧w = 1 iff Minw(⟦A⟧) ⊆ ⟦C⟧.
Might-counterfactual⟦A ◇→ C⟧w = 1 iff Minw(⟦A⟧) ∩ ⟦C⟧ ≠ ∅.

Model. A match. The actual world is w0. Rank 1 is closest to w0 after w0 itself.

Example

If the match had been struck, it would have lit.

⟦struck⟧ = {w1, w3, w4, w5}. Lowest rank among them: 1. Minw0(⟦struck⟧) = {w1}.

w1 ∈ ⟦lights⟧. So {w1} ⊆ ⟦lights⟧. True at w0.

Truth in fiction

Definitions
In f, φφ is true in the fiction f.
Told as known factTf: the worlds where f is told as a report of known fact.
Analysis 0⟦In f, φ⟧w = 1 iff Tf ⊆ ⟦φ⟧.
Analysis 1⟦In f, φ⟧w = 1 iff Minw(Tf) ⊆ ⟦φ⟧. Vacuously true if Tf = ∅.
Reality PrincipleWalton’s name for the idea behind Analysis 1. The world of the story is as like the actual world as the story allows.
Collective belief worldsCBf(w): the worlds where everything overtly believed in the community where f was told is true.
Analysis 2⟦In f, φ⟧w = 1 iff Minw′(Tf) ⊆ ⟦φ⟧ for every w′ ∈ CBf(w).
Mutual Belief PrincipleWalton’s name for the idea behind Analysis 2. The world of the story is as like what the community where it was told believed as the story allows.
Explicit, implicit truthTrue in f and stated in it; true in f without being stated.
IncompletenessFor some φ, neither In f, φ nor In f, ¬φ is true.

Model. f is The Lord of the Rings. Tf = {t1, t2, t3, t4}. The actual world is w0 and CBf(w0) = {b0}. h: Frodo cannot breathe under water. e: Sam has an even number of hairs on his left foot. v: Frodo throws the Ring into the fire. z: Hobbiton lies on chalk. Narrators

Definitions
NarratorThe teller of a story, inside the fiction.
Unreliable narratorA narrator whose statements are not all true in the story.
Authorial authorityThe principle that what the text of a fiction states is true in the fiction. Unreliable narrators are counterexamples.
Free indirect discourseA character’s thought given without a verb of thinking, in the narrator’s tense and person.
Context of utteranceThe narrator’s context. It fixes tense and pronouns.
Context of thoughtThe character’s context. In free indirect discourse it fixes now, today, tomorrow and exclamations.

Data. The first edition of The Hobbit (1937) tells that Gollum promised Bilbo a present if he lost the riddle game, and that the present was a ring. The Prologue to The Lord of the Rings (1954) says that this was the account Bilbo gave his friends and wrote in his diary, a lie, and that in fact Bilbo found the Ring and Gollum never offered it to him.

Fictional names

Definitions
Fictional useA name used in telling a story. Pretence; it refers to nothing.
Metafictional useA name used for a character as a cultural artefact. Holmes was created by Conan Doyle.
Parafictional useA name used to report what holds in a story, with in the story said or left silent.
Mixed discourseTalk that moves between parafictional and metafictional uses of the same name, as in (28).
Negative existentialA sentence of the form N does not exist.
PossibilismFictional characters are merely possible objects. Holmes exists at the worlds where the stories are told as known fact.
MeinongianismFictional characters are objects that do not exist.
CreationismFictional characters are abstract artefacts, created by their authors.
Pretence theoryThere are no fictional characters. Talk of them is make-believe, or reports what is true in make-believe.

Fake past

Definitions
Topic timeThe time a sentence is about.
Fake pastPast morphology that does not place the eventuality before the time of utterance.
Exclusion featureT(x) excludes C(x). T(x): the x we are talking about. C(x): the x that for all we know is the speaker’s. x ranges over times or worlds.
Epic preteriteThe past tense of narrative fiction. It combines with present and future adverbs.
Konjunktiv IIThe German past subjunctive: hätte, wäre.
  1. (31)If Mia had a dog, she would be happy.English
  2. (32)Morgen war Weihnachten.German · tomorrow be.pst.3sg Christmas · ‘Tomorrow was Christmas.’
  3. (33)Wenn Mia einen Hund hätte, wäre sie glücklich.German · if Mia a dog have.sbjv.pst, be.sbjv.pst she happy · ‘If Mia had a dog, she would be happy.’
  4. (34)Io ero il papà che stava a sega’ per finta.Italian, Rome · ero be.ipfv.1sg, stava be.ipfv.3sg · ‘I’m the daddy who’s sawing, pretend.’
  5. (35)Moi j’étais le papa, et toi tu étais la maman.French · étais be.ipfv.1sg/2sg · ‘I’m the daddy, and you’re the mummy.’
  6. (36)Tää olis prinsessa ja tässä olis sen linna.Finnish · olis be.cond.3sg · ‘This is a princess, and here is her castle.’
