Conversational backgrounds

  1. (1)Mary must leave.
  2. (2)In view of what I know, Mary must leave.
  3. (3)In view of the rules of the secret committee, Mary must leave.

(2) is epistemic and (3) is deontic, with the same must. Kratzer’s proposal: modals are not (primarily) lexically ambiguous. The differences among epistemic, deontic, circumstantial, teleological and bouletic uses of a modal come from contextually supplied background assumptions, conversational backgrounds. An in view of phrase names the background; in (1) the context supplies it.

Definitions
Conversational backgroundA function f from worlds to sets of propositions, f : W → ℘(℘(W)). f(w) is a set of propositions (facts, rules, goals, circumstances, …) relevant at w.
∩f(w)The intersection of the propositions in f(w): the worlds where all of them are true.
Accessibility from fwRfw′ iff w′ ∈ ∩f(w).
Entails, compatibleA set of propositions Σ entails p iff ∩Σ ⊆ p. Σ is compatible with p iff ∩Σ ∩ p ≠ ∅.
must φTrue at w iff f(w) entails ⟦φ⟧: ∩f(w) ⊆ ⟦φ⟧.
might φTrue at w iff f(w) is compatible with ⟦φ⟧: ∩f(w) ∩ ⟦φ⟧ ≠ ∅.
In view of αSets the conversational background: ⟦in view of α, S⟧w,f = ⟦S⟧w,⟦α⟧.
  1. (4)#Sue knows that it is raining, but it isn’t.
  2. (5)You must be home by six, but you won’t be.
  3. (6)In view of the rules, you must leave and you must stay.

What Sue knows is true at the actual world: (4) is a contradiction. The rules need not be obeyed there: (5) is fine. (6) describes rules that cannot all be obeyed. Each is a property of f.

Ordering semantics

  1. (7)[the speaker has done wrong before and will again] I must not throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.
  2. (8)I must throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.

Put the moral principles in f(w). The facts include wrongs already done, so no world obeys every principle: ∩f(w) = ∅, and (7) and (8) both come out true. Yet a world where I throw the cup is clearly worse than one where I don’t. A single accessibility relation makes a binary cut: accessible and inaccessible worlds. Kratzer keeps the facts in one conversational background and uses the principles, in a second, to rank the worlds.

Definitions
Modal baseA conversational background f fixing which worlds are relevant: those in ∩f(w).
Ordering sourceA conversational background g. g(w) is a set of ideals at w: laws, priorities, goals, stereotypical expectations.
u ≤g(w) viff {p ∈ g(w) : v ∈ p} ⊆ {p ∈ g(w) : u ∈ p}. u satisfies every ideal that v satisfies: u is at least as good as v.
u <g(w) vu ≤g(w) v, and u satisfies some ideal that v does not: u is better than v.
Best(f(w), g(w)){u ∈ ∩f(w) : there is no v ∈ ∩f(w) with v <g(w) u}.
must φTrue at w iff Best(f(w), g(w)) ⊆ ⟦φ⟧.
might φTrue at w iff Best(f(w), g(w)) ∩ ⟦φ⟧ ≠ ∅.
Limit assumptionThere always are best worlds. It holds in every finite model; Kratzer (1981) does not assume it.

This keeps the universal and existential forces of block 0, but replaces ‘all accessible worlds’ with ‘all best accessible worlds’. With an empty ordering source, Best(f(w), g(w)) = ∩f(w).

Worked: Logan
  1. p = Logan does his homework. The circumstances leave open whether he does it.
  2. f(w): the relevant circumstances. Logan has maths today; Logan has time tonight.
  3. g(w): the salient priorities. Logan follows the teacher’s rules; Logan does required assignments.
  4. Logan must do his homework is true iff Logan does his homework in all the best f-worlds, ranked by g. Logan might do his homework is true iff he does it in some of them.

Model. W = {w0, w1, w2, w3}. f(w0) = {A, B} and g(w0) = {I1, I2}, where A = {w0, w1, w2}, B = {w0, w1, w3}, I1 = {w0, w1} and I2 = {w0, w2}.

Modal bases and ordering sources

  1. (9)[soil and climate suit hydrangeas; we know none are planted here] Hydrangeas can grow here.
  2. (10)[the same garden] There might be hydrangeas growing here.
  3. (11)[a doctor, after the examination] You must have the flu.
  4. (12)You have the flu.

(9) is true and (10) is false of the same garden: (9) depends on the circumstances, (10) on what we know. These are the two kinds of modal base. (11) is weaker than (12): the doctor’s conclusion also rests on expectations about how illnesses usually go, and those can fail. Expectations like these are an ordering source.

Modal bases
Epistemicf(w) is a set of facts known in w.
Circumstantialf(w) is a set of circumstances holding in w.
Ordering sources
Deonticg(w) is a set of rules in force in w.
Bouleticg(w) is a set of desires in w.
Teleologicalg(w) is a set of goals in w.
Stereotypicalg(w) is a set of expectations concerning what w is like.
Doxasticg(w) is a set of beliefs in w.
Emptyg(w) = ∅: no world is better than any other.

Graded modality

  1. (17)It is probable that Anna is home, but she might not be.
  2. (18)#Anna must be home, but she might not be.
  3. (19)There is a slight possibility that Anna is home.
  4. (20)It is more likely that Anna is home than that she is at work.

(17) and (18): probable is weaker than must, and it is stronger than might. (19) suggests that Anna is probably not home. (20) compares two propositions. ∀ and ∃ over one set of worlds give only two forces; Kratzer (1981, 1991) defines the others from the ordering. The definitions do not assume that best worlds exist. All worlds below are in ∩f(w), and ≤ is ≤g(w).

Definitions
Necessityp is a necessity iff for every u there is a v ≤ u such that every z ≤ v is in p. In a finite model: Best(f(w), g(w)) ⊆ p.
Possibility¬p is not a necessity.
Slight possibilityp is compatible with f(w), and ¬p is a necessity.
Good possibilityThere is a u such that every v ≤ u is in p.
Better possibilityp is a better possibility than q iff (i) for every q-world u there is a p-world v ≤ u, and (ii) some p-world u has no q-world v ≤ u.
Weak necessityp is a better possibility than ¬p.

Deontic paradoxes

Definitions
Samaritan paradoxAn obligation to help the victim of a wrong, formalised in standard deontic logic, entails an obligation that the wrong occur.
Chisholm’s paradoxFour consistent sentences about an obligation and what to do if it is broken entail, in standard deontic logic, both □q and □¬q.
  1. (21)It ought to be that John is not injured in a robbery.
  2. (22)It ought to be that someone helps John, who was injured in a robbery.
  3. (23)It ought to be that John is injured in a robbery and someone helps John.
Åqvist’s formalisation
  1. p: John was injured in a robbery. q: someone helps John.
  2. (21) is □¬p. (22) is □(p ∧ q).
  3. □(p ∧ q) entails □p. With □¬p this gives □p ∧ □¬p, which D rules out.
Kratzer’s solution
  1. (21) is interpreted with a realistic modal base f that does not entail p, and a deontic ordering source that ranks worlds without robbery above otherwise similar worlds with one.
  2. The relative clause in (22) presents p as given. So (22) is interpreted with a modal base f′ with p ∈ f′(w), and the same ordering source ranks worlds where someone helps John above worlds where nobody does.
  3. (21) and (22) are true relative to different modal bases, so they are consistent.

Model. Three worlds: at w1 John is not injured and nobody helps him; at w2 he is injured and someone helps him; at w3 he is injured and nobody helps him. ∩f(w) = {w1, w2, w3}; ∩f′(w) = {w2, w3}. g(w) = {¬p, p → q}.

(b)Show that (21) is true relative to f, and that someone helps John is a necessity relative to f′.

  2. (25)Mary robs John.
  3. (26)If Mary robs John, she must be punished.
  4. (27)If Mary does not rob John, she must not be punished.
(d)An if-clause adds its proposition to the modal base. Which two of (24) to (27) together give Mary must be punished, and which two give Mary must not be punished? Say which modal base each conclusion is drawn under.
