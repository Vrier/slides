Ordering sources

  1. (1)[the speaker has done wrong before and will again] I must not throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.
  2. (2)I must throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.

Put the moral principles in f(w). Wrongs are already done, so no world obeys them all: ∩f(w) = ∅, and (1) and (2) both come out true. Kratzer’s fix: keep the facts in f(w) and use the principles to rank the worlds.

Definitions
Modal basef. Which worlds are relevant: those in ∩f(w).
Ordering sourceg. g(w) is a set of ideals (rules, goals, expectations) used to rank those worlds.
At least as goodu ≤g(w) v: u satisfies every ideal that v satisfies.
Betteru <g(w) v: u is at least as good as v, and satisfies some ideal that v does not.

Model. g(w) holds three house rules. Five worlds in ∩f(w):

Best worlds

  1. (3)[the circumstances leave open whether he does it; the teacher’s rules require it] Logan must do his homework.

Some worlds compatible with the circumstances have Logan skipping his homework. (3) is still true: he does it in the worlds that rank highest by the rules.

Definitions
Best(f(w), g(w))The worlds in ∩f(w) that no other world in ∩f(w) is better than.
must φTrue iff φ is true in every best world.
might φTrue iff φ is true in some best world.
No ordering sourceNo world is better than another, so every world in ∩f(w) is best: the week 2 system.

Model. W = {w0, w1, w2, w3}. ∩f(w) = {w0, w1}. g(w) = {I1, I2}, with I1 = {w0, w1} and I2 = {w0, w2}.

2

Chisholm’s paradox

  1. (4)Mary must not rob John.
  2. (5)Mary robs John.
  3. (6)If Mary robs John, she must be punished.
  4. (7)It must be the case that if Mary does not rob John, she is not punished.

Exercise. Let r be Mary robs John and q be Mary is punished.

(a)Translate each of (4) to (7) into modal logic.
(b)Use (5) and (6) to derive □q, and (4) and (7) to derive □¬q. Why is that a problem?
Kratzer’s solution
  1. An if-clause adds its proposition to the modal base.
  2. (4) and (7) use the modal base f. The main clause of (6) uses f plus r.
  3. The ordering source ranks the worlds in both.

Model. Three worlds in ∩f(w): at w1 Mary does not rob John and is not punished; at w2 she robs him and is punished; at w3 she robs him and is not punished. g(w) = {Mary does not rob John, robbers are punished}.

(c)Which world is best relative to f? Show that (4) and (7) are true.
(d)Which world is best once r is added to f? Show that the main clause of (6) is true. Why is there no contradiction?

Chisholm 1963. Contrary-to-duty imperatives and deontic logic. Analysis 24: 33–36.

Modal bases and ordering sources

  1. (8)[soil and climate suit hydrangeas; I know none grow on my land] Hydrangeas can grow here.
  2. (9)[the same land] There might be hydrangeas growing here.
  3. (10)[a doctor, after the examination] You must have the flu.
  4. (11)You have the flu.

(8) is true and (9) false of the same land: (8) depends on the circumstances, (9) on what I know. Two kinds of modal base. (10) is weaker than (11): the doctor also relies on how illnesses usually go. Expectations like these are an ordering source.

Modal bases
Epistemicf(w): facts known in w.
Circumstantialf(w): circumstances holding in w.
Ordering sources

Bouleticdesires.
Teleologicalgoals.
Stereotypicalexpectations about what is normal.

Graded modality

  1. (16)It is probable that Mary is lost, but she might not be.
  2. (17)# Mary must be lost, but she might not be.
  3. (18)There is a slight possibility that Mary is lost.
  4. (19)There is a good possibility that Mary is lost.
  5. (20)It is more likely that Mary doesn’t want to come to your party than that she is lost.

With only ∀ and ∃ over one set of worlds there are just two forces. Kratzer (1981, 1991) uses the ordering to define more.

Force and flavour across languages

  1. (21)[a teacher, at the end of an exam] You may leave now.
  2. (22)[looking at the sky] It may rain later.

One may, two flavours, the same force. In English the word fixes the force and the context supplies the flavour.
