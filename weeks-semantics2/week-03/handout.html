Ordering Semantics

Modal bases. Modals are not lexically ambiguous. Their flavour comes from a contextually supplied conversational background f, and must and might quantify over the worlds in ∩f(w). Gradability (and Chisholm’s paradox, a.o.) demonstrate that we need something more than this. Kratzer proposes ordering semantics to solve this.

  1. (1)[the speaker has done wrong before and will again] I must not throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.
  2. (2)I must throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.

A single accessibility relation makes a binary cut: accessible vs. inaccessible worlds. Put the moral principles in f(w): wrongs are already done, so no world obeys them all, ∩f(w) = ∅, and (1) and (2) both come out true. Many modal judgements, especially deontic and teleological ones, need graded comparison among worlds. Kratzer models this with two conversational backgrounds.

Definitions
Conversational backgroundA function from worlds to sets of propositions. f(w) is a set of propositions (facts, rules, goals, circumstances, …) relevant at w.
Modal basef: which worlds are relevant. Those in ∩f(w).
Ordering sourceg: which of those worlds are better in view of norms, ideals or goals. g(w) is a set of ideal propositions at w.
At least as goodu ≤g(w) v iff u satisfies every ideal in g(w) that v satisfies. Intuition: u does at least as well by the ideals as v.
Betteru <g(w) v: u is at least as good as v, and satisfies some ideal that v does not.

Best worlds

  1. (6)[Howard returned his library book late] Howard has to pay a fine.
  2. (7)Howard may leave without paying.
  3. (8)Howard has to return the book on time.
Definitions
Best(f(w), g(w))The worlds in ∩f(w) that no other world in ∩f(w) is better than.
must φTrue iff φ is true in every best world.
might φTrue iff φ is true in some best world.
Empty ordering sourceg(w) = ∅. No world is better than another, so every world in ∩f(w) is best. Equivalent to no ordering source. Note that this predicts no gradability.

This keeps modal force from before, universal or existential, but replaces “all accessible worlds” with “all best accessible worlds”. The choice of f and g is what yields deontic vs. epistemic vs. teleological readings, without building them in as lexical ambiguities of the modal.

A small worked example
  1. Let p = Howard pays a fine.
  2. The modal base f(w) encodes the circumstances: Howard returned the book late. So every world in ∩f(w) is a late-return world.
  3. The ordering source g(w) encodes the library’s rules: no book is returned late; anyone who returns a book late pays a fine.
  4. No world in ∩f(w) keeps the first rule. The best are those that keep the second: Howard pays.
  5. (6) is true iff Howard pays in all the best worlds. It is.

Model. Three worlds: at w0 Howard returns the book on time; at w1 he returns it late and pays the fine; at w2 he returns it late and does not pay. The facts: ∩f(w) = {w1, w2}. g(w) = {no book is returned late, anyone who returns a book late pays a fine}.

Chisholm’s paradox

  1. (9)Mary must not rob John.□¬r
  2. (10)Mary robs John.r
  3. (11)If Mary robs John, she must be punished.r → □q
  4. (12)It must be the case that if Mary does not rob John, she is not punished.□(¬r → ¬q)

r: Mary robs John. q: Mary is punished.

Exercise 3 (Chisholm).

(a)Use (10) and (11) to derive □q, and (9) and (12) to derive □¬q. Why is that a problem?
Kratzer’s solution
  1. An if-clause adds its proposition to the modal base.
  2. (9) and (12) use the modal base f, which leaves the robbery open. With (10) in f, (9) could not be said.
  3. The main clause of (11) uses f plus r.
  4. The ordering source ranks the worlds in both.

Model. Three worlds in ∩f(w): at w1 Mary does not rob John and is not punished; at w2 she robs him and is punished; at w3 she robs him and is not punished. g(w) = {Mary does not rob John, robbers are punished, non-robbers are not punished}.

(b)Which world is best relative to f? Show that (9) and (12) are true.
(c)Which world is best once r is added to f? Show that the main clause of (11) is true. Why is there no contradiction?

Graded modality

  1. (23)It is probable that Mary is lost, but she might not be.
  2. (24)# Mary must be lost, but she might not be.
  3. (25)There is a slight possibility that Mary is lost.
  4. (26)It is more likely that Mary doesn’t want to come to your party than that she is lost.

(23) and (24): probable is weaker than must. (25) suggests Mary is probably not lost. (26) compares two propositions. With only ∀ and ∃ over one set of worlds there are just two forces. Kratzer (1981, 1991) uses the ordering to define more.

Definitions, in a finite model
Slight possibilityp is true in some world in ∩f(w), but in no best world.
More likelyp is more likely than q iff (i) every q-world in ∩f(w) has a p-world at least as good as it, and (ii) some p-world has no q-world at least as good as it.
Probablep is more likely than ¬p.

What you need to know

Key notions
  1. Conversational backgrounds: modal base f and ordering source g.
  2. Epistemic and circumstantial modal bases; deontic, bouletic, teleological and stereotypical ordering sources.
  3. Best worlds, Best(f(w), g(w)).
  4. Chisholm’s paradox; graded modality.
  5. Lexical entries for must and might; IFA.
Skills
  1. Rank worlds by a set of ideals, and find the best ones.
  2. Draw ∩f(w) and the best worlds from a definition of f and g.
  3. Given a model, decide whether a modal sentence is true against the best worlds.
  4. Describe a plausible modal base and ordering source for a modal in context.
  5. Explain how Kratzer’s system avoids Chisholm’s paradox.
  6. Compute must, might, slight possibility and probability in a small model.
  7. Compute a modal sentence node by node in a tree.
