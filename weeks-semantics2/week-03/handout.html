Ordering sources

  1. (1)[the speaker has done wrong before and will again] I must not throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.
  2. (2)I must throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.

Put the moral principles in f(w). Wrongs are already done, so no world obeys them all: ∩f(w) = ∅, and (1) and (2) both come out true. Kratzer’s fix: keep the facts in f(w) and use the principles to rank the worlds.

Definitions
Modal basef. Which worlds are relevant: those in ∩f(w).
Ordering sourceg. g(w) is a set of ideals (rules, goals, expectations) used to rank those worlds.
At least as goodu ≤g(w) v: u satisfies every ideal that v satisfies.
Betteru <g(w) v: u is at least as good as v, and satisfies some ideal that v does not.

The lattice from the lecture, for three house rules in g(w): p, no shoes indoors; q, dishes done; r, quiet after eleven. Arrows point to better worlds.

1

Best worlds

  1. (6)[Howard returned his library book late] Howard has to pay a fine.
  2. (7)Howard may leave without paying.
  3. (8)Howard has to return the book on time.

The modal base fixes that the book came back late. The library’s rules prefer worlds where no book is late, and among the rest, worlds where the fine is paid. (6) is true: Howard pays in the best worlds the facts allow.

Definitions
Best(f(w), g(w))The worlds in ∩f(w) that no other world in ∩f(w) is better than.
must φTrue iff φ is true in every best world.
might φTrue iff φ is true in some best world.
No ordering sourceNo world is better than another, so every world in ∩f(w) is best: the week 2 system.

Model. Three worlds: at w0 Howard returns the book on time; at w1 he returns it late and pays the fine; at w2 he returns it late and does not pay. The facts: ∩f(w) = {w1, w2}. g(w) = {no book is returned late, anyone who returns a book late pays a fine}.

2

Chisholm’s paradox

  1. (9)Mary must not rob John.
  2. (10)Mary robs John.
  3. (11)If Mary robs John, she must be punished.
  4. (12)It must be the case that if Mary does not rob John, she is not punished.

Exercise. Let r be Mary robs John and q be Mary is punished.

(a)Translate each of (9) to (12) into modal logic.
(b)Use (10) and (11) to derive □q, and (9) and (12) to derive □¬q. Why is that a problem?
Kratzer’s solution
  1. An if-clause adds its proposition to the modal base.
  2. (9) and (12) use the modal base f, which leaves the robbery open. With (10) in f, (9) could not be said.
  3. The main clause of (11) uses f plus r.
  4. The ordering source ranks the worlds in both.

Model. Three worlds in ∩f(w): at w1 Mary does not rob John and is not punished; at w2 she robs him and is punished; at w3 she robs him and is not punished. g(w) = {Mary does not rob John, robbers are punished, non-robbers are not punished}.

(c)Which world is best relative to f? Show that (9) and (12) are true.
(d)Which world is best once r is added to f? Show that the main clause of (11) is true. Why is there no contradiction?

Chisholm 1963. Contrary-to-duty imperatives and deontic logic. Analysis 24: 33–36.

Modal bases and ordering sources

  1. (13)[soil and climate suit hydrangeas; I know none grow on my land] Hydrangeas can grow here.
  2. (14)[the same land] There might be hydrangeas growing here.
  3. (15)[a doctor, after the examination] You must have the flu.
  4. (16)You have the flu.

(13) is true and (14) false of the same land: (13) depends on the circumstances, (14) on what I know. Two kinds of modal base. (15) is weaker than (16): the doctor also relies on how illnesses usually go. Expectations like these are an ordering source (Kratzer 1991). This weakness is contested: von Fintel & Gillies (2010) argue that must is strong and only signals indirect evidence.

Epistemicf(w): facts known in w.
Circumstantialf(w): circumstances holding in w.

Graded modality

  1. (22)It is probable that Mary is lost, but she might not be.
  2. (23)# Mary must be lost, but she might not be.
  3. (24)There is a slight possibility that Mary is lost.
  4. (25)There is a good possibility that Mary is lost.
  5. (26)It is more likely that Mary doesn’t want to come to your party than that she is lost.

With only ∀ and ∃ over one set of worlds there are just two forces. Kratzer (1981, 1991) uses the ordering to define more.

Force and flavour across languages

  1. (27)[a teacher, at the end of an exam] You may leave now.
  2. (28)[looking at the sky] It may rain later.
  3. (29)[the lights are on] He must be home by now.
  4. (30)[a parent to a teenager] You must be home by ten.
  5. (31)[a teacher] You can go now.
  6. (32)This car can reach 200 km an hour.

(27) and (28): one may, two flavours, the same force. In English the word fixes the force and the context supplies the flavour.
