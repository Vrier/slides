Ordering sources

  1. (1)[the speaker has done wrong before and will again] I must not throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.
  2. (2)I must throw my coffee cup at the student outside my office.

Put the moral principles in f(w). The facts include wrongs already done, so no world obeys every principle: ∩f(w) = ∅, and (1) and (2) both come out true. Yet a world where I throw the cup is clearly worse than one where I don’t. A single accessibility relation makes a binary cut: accessible and inaccessible worlds. Kratzer keeps the facts in one conversational background and uses the principles, in a second, to rank the worlds.

Definitions
Modal baseA conversational background f fixing which worlds are relevant: those in ∩f(w).
Ordering sourceA conversational background g. g(w) is a set of ideals at w: laws, priorities, goals, stereotypical expectations.
u ≤g(w) viff {p ∈ g(w) : v ∈ p} ⊆ {p ∈ g(w) : u ∈ p}. u satisfies every ideal that v satisfies: u is at least as good as v.
u <g(w) vu ≤g(w) v, and u satisfies some ideal that v does not: u is better than v.
IncomparableNeither u ≤g(w) v nor v ≤g(w) u. So the order is partial.

Model. g(w) holds three house rules: p, no shoes indoors; q, dishes done; r, quiet after eleven. Five worlds in ∩f(w):

Best worlds

  1. (3)[the circumstances leave open whether he does it; the teacher’s rules require it] Logan must do his homework.

The circumstances include worlds where Logan skips his homework. (3) is still true: in the worlds that rank highest by the rules, he does it.

Definitions
Best(f(w), g(w)){u ∈ ∩f(w) : there is no v ∈ ∩f(w) with v <g(w) u}.
must φTrue at w iff Best(f(w), g(w)) ⊆ ⟦φ⟧.
might φTrue at w iff Best(f(w), g(w)) ∩ ⟦φ⟧ ≠ ∅.
Empty ordering sourceg(w) = ∅. No world is better than any other, so Best(f(w), g(w)) = ∩f(w).
Limit assumptionThere always are best worlds. It holds in every finite model; Kratzer (1981) does not assume it.

Model. W = {w0, w1, w2, w3}. f(w0) = {A, B} and g(w0) = {I1, I2}, where A = {w0, w1, w2}, B = {w0, w1, w3}, I1 = {w0, w1} and I2 = {w0, w2}.

Exercise.

(a)Compute ∩f(w0).
(b)Compute Best(f(w0), g(w0)).
(c)Let p = {w0, w2, w3}. Is must p true at w0? Is might p?
(d)Replace g(w0) with {I1, I3}, where I3 = {w1}. Recompute Best, and show that must ¬p is true at w0 although p is true at w0. Which axiom fails? Is f realistic at w0?

Chisholm’s paradox

  1. (4)Mary must not rob John.
  2. (5)Mary robs John.
  3. (6)If Mary robs John, she must be punished.
  4. (7)It must be the case that if Mary does not rob John, she is not punished.

Each sentence is plausible, and the four sound consistent together.

Exercise. Let r be Mary robs John and q be Mary is punished.

(a)Translate each of (4) to (7) into modal logic.
(b)Show that in standard deontic logic (KD) they entail □q ∧ □¬q. Which axiom rules this out?
Kratzer’s solution
  1. An if-clause adds its proposition to the modal base.
  2. (4) and (7) are interpreted with the modal base f; the main clause of (6) with f plus r.
  3. One ordering source ranks the worlds throughout: no robbery is best, robbery and punishment next, robbery unpunished worst.

Model. Four worlds in ∩f(w): at w1 Mary does not rob John and is not punished; at w2 she robs him and is punished; at w3 she robs him and is not punished; at w4 she does not rob him and is punished. g(w) = {¬r, r → q, ¬r → ¬q}.

(c)Compute Best(f(w), g(w)), and Best for the modal base f plus r.
(d)Show that (4) and (7) are true relative to f, and that she is punished is a necessity relative to f plus r.
(e)Relative to which modal base is Mary must not be punished true, and relative to which is Mary must be punished true? Why is there no contradiction?

Modal bases and ordering sources

  1. (8)[soil and climate suit hydrangeas; I know none grow on my land] Hydrangeas can grow here.
  2. (9)[the same land] There might be hydrangeas growing here.
  3. (10)[a doctor, after the examination] You must have the flu.
  4. (11)You have the flu.

(8) is true and (9) false of the same land: (8) depends on the circumstances, (9) on what I know. These are the two kinds of modal base. (10) is weaker than (11): the doctor’s conclusion also rests on expectations about how illnesses usually go, and those can fail. Expectations like these are an ordering source.

Modal bases
Epistemicf(w) is a set of facts known in w.
Circumstantialf(w) is a set of circumstances holding in w.
Ordering sources
Deonticg(w) is a set of rules in force in w.
Bouleticg(w) is a set of desires in w.
Teleologicalg(w) is a set of goals in w.
Stereotypicalg(w) is a set of expectations concerning what w is like.
Doxasticg(w) is a set of beliefs in w.
Emptyg(w) = ∅.

Graded modality

  1. (16)It is probable that Mary is lost, but she might not be.
  2. (17)# Mary must be lost, but she might not be.
  3. (18)There is a slight possibility that Mary is lost.
  4. (19)It is more likely that Mary doesn’t want to come to your party than that she is lost.

(16) and (17): probable is weaker than must. (18) suggests Mary is probably not lost. (19) compares two propositions. ∀ and ∃ over one set of worlds give only two forces; Kratzer (1981, 1991) defines the others from the ordering. The definitions do not assume that best worlds exist. All worlds below are in ∩f(w), and ≤ is ≤g(w).

Definitions
Necessityp is a necessity iff for every u there is a v ≤ u such that every z ≤ v is in p. In a finite model: Best(f(w), g(w)) ⊆ p.
Possibility¬p is not a necessity.
Slight possibilityp is compatible with f(w), and ¬p is a necessity.
Good possibilityThere is a u such that every v ≤ u is in p.
Better possibilityp is a better possibility than q iff (i) for every q-world u there is a p-world v ≤ u, and (ii) some p-world u has no q-world v ≤ u.
Weak necessityp is a better possibility than ¬p.

Force and flavour across languages

  1. (20)[a teacher, at the end of an exam] You may leave now.
  2. (21)[looking at the sky] It may rain later.

One may, two flavours, the same force. In English the word fixes the force and the context supplies the flavour.

  1. (22)planalreadyk’ainfertu7thenqwatsátsleave‘He must have left by now.’
  2. (23)[His car isn’t there.]planalreadyk’ainferqwatsátsleave‘Maybe he’s already gone.’

St’át’imcets (Lillooet Salish): the same epistemic k’a in both. Here the word fixes the flavour (deontic ka, epistemic k’a, future kelh) and the context supplies the force.

Modals and attitude verbs, compositionally

  1. (24)In view of the rules of the secret committee, Mary must leave.
  2. (25)Mary might leave.
  3. (26)Sue believes that it is raining.
  4. (27)Sue knows that it is raining.
  5. (28)[epistemic must] Sue must know that it is raining.

A first pass. The modal and the attitude verbs all quantify over worlds. The modal takes its conversational backgrounds as arguments; the verbs fix theirs through their subject.

What you need to know

After this week you can
  1. Say what an ordering source adds to a modal base, and rank worlds by a set of ideals.
  2. Compute Best(f(w), g(w)) in a small model and evaluate must and might.
  3. Explain how an ordering source makes must p false where p is true, and why T can fail with a realistic modal base.
  4. Formalise Chisholm’s sentences, derive the paradox, and resolve it with modal bases and an ordering source.
  5. Describe a plausible modal base and ordering source for a modal in context.
  6. Classify a proposition as a necessity, weak necessity, possibility, good, slight or better possibility in a small finite model.
  7. Say whether a language’s modals fix force or flavour, from data.
  8. Give lexical entries for must, might, believe and know, and derive truth conditions with them.
