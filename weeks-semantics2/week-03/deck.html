Four readings of \(\Box\)

\(\Box p\) is true at \(w\) if \(p\) is true at every world \(w\) sees; \(\Diamond p\) if at some. Each reading puts English words in place of \(\Box\) and \(\Diamond\).

T: \(\Box p \rightarrow p\). Which hold whatever the facts
(1)
If the shop is always open, it is open now.
(2)
If Sue knows it is raining, it is raining.
(3)
If you must be home by six, you are home by six.
(4)
If it must be raining, it is raining.
Recap

T: \(\Box p \rightarrow p\)

(1)holds
The present is one of the times always covers. Reflexive.
(2)holds
Knowledge is factive. Reflexive.
(3)fails
Rules can be broken. Not reflexive.
(4)predicted to hold
But it must be raining is odd when you can see the rain. The problem from week 2.
D: \(\Box p \rightarrow \Diamond p\). Which hold whatever the facts
(5)
If the shop is always open, it is sometimes open.
(6)
If Sue knows it is raining, then for all she knows it is raining.
(7)
If you must leave, you may leave.
(8)
If it must be raining, it might be raining.
Recap

D: \(\Box p \rightarrow \Diamond p\)

(5) to (8) all hold. Every world sees at least one world: serial.
(7): an obligation that you are not permitted to carry out would make no sense.
D is weaker than T. Rules satisfy D but not T.
4: \(\Box p \rightarrow \Box\Box p\)  ·  5: \(\Diamond p \rightarrow \Box\Diamond p\)
(9)  4
If the shop is always open, it is always the case that it is always open.
(10) 4
If Sue knows it is raining, she knows that she knows it.
(11) 5
If the shop is sometimes open, it is always the case that it is sometimes open.
(12) 5
If for all Sue knows it is raining, she knows that for all she knows it is raining.
Recap

4 and 5

(9)4 · holds
A time later than a later time is itself later. Transitive.
(10)4 · contested
The KK principle. Hintikka for it; Williamson against.
(11)5 · depends
If always covers all times, it holds. If it covers the future only, it fails once the shop closes for good.
(12)5 · fails
Sue can be unaware of what she does not know. Negative introspection.

Empirical

Graded modality

(16) > (17) > (18) > (19) > (20). (21) compares two propositions.
Slight possibility is negative: (20) suggests that Mary is probably not lost.
A set of accessible worlds only gives \(\forall\) and \(\exists\). The grades in between need a way to rank worlds against each other.
Kratzer (1991): necessity, weak necessity, good possibility, possibility, slight possibility, better possibility.

Weak must

After von Fintel & Gillies (2010)
(22)Watching the rain through the window#It must be raining.
(23)Wet coats in the hallIt must be raining.
Karttunen (1972): must p sounds weaker than plain p.
Last week’s theory: reflexive \(R\), so must p entails p. It says nothing about evidence.
von Fintel & Gillies (2010): must is strong, and signals indirect evidence.
Put all four into last week’s logic
(24)
Mary must not rob John.
(25)
Mary robs John.
(26)

(27)

Chisholm (1963), in the version of Åqvist (1967)
Empirical

Chisholm’s paradox

\(r\): Mary robs John  ·  \(q\): Mary is punished  ·  after Åqvist (1967)
(24)\(\Box\neg r\)
(25)\(r\)
(26)\(r \rightarrow \Box q\)
(27)\(\Box(\neg r \rightarrow \neg q)\)
(25) and (26): \(\Box q\). (24), (27) and K: \(\Box\neg q\). Together \(\Box q \wedge \Box\neg q\), which D rules out.
Intuitively there are three grades of world: no robbery is best, robbery and punishment next, robbery without punishment worst. An accessibility relation only separates accessible from inaccessible worlds.

Modal semantics from 1973 to 1991.
Historical

David Lewis

David Lewis
1941 – 2001
Princeton.
1973. Counterfactuals. Worlds ordered by similarity to ours. If A were, C would be: some A-and-C world is closer than any A-and-not-C world.
Counterfactuals also treats conditional obligation, ranking worlds by comparative goodness.
1975. “Adverbs of quantification”: an if-clause restricts a quantifier (week 8).
1981. “Ordering semantics and premise semantics for counterfactuals”: Kratzer’s premise sets induce an ordering, and the two semantics are equivalent.

Historical

Against ambiguity

Modal logic for linguists. Epistemic must and deontic must are two words, each with its own \(R\).
Kratzer. There is one must, and the context supplies the background.
The evidence. The same range of readings shows up with must, may, can, should and with German müssen, können. In view of phrases fix the reading compositionally. Deontic modals regularly develop epistemic uses over time.
On an ambiguity account, each of these would be a coincidence.

Conversational backgrounds

Kratzer (1991)
(28)All Maori children must learn the names of their ancestors.
(29)The ancestors of the Maori must have arrived from Tahiti.
(30)If you must sneeze, at least use your handkerchief.
One must. What changes is the background: the laws in (28), what we know in (29), your bodily dispositions in (30).
Conversational background. A function \(f\) from worlds to sets of propositions: \(f(w) \subseteq \wp(W)\).
Formal

Intersecting a background

Example. What I know, for week 2’s Anna must be home: \(f(w)\) = {the lights are on, Anna’s car is outside, her keys are on the hook}.
\(\bigcap f(w)\): the worlds where every proposition in \(f(w)\) is true.
Formal

From \(f\) to \(R\)

The relation. \(wR_f w'\) iff \(w' \in \bigcap f(w)\).
Last week
\(\sem{\textit{must}}^{w} = \lambda R.\,\lambda p.\;\forall w'\,[\,wRw' \rightarrow w' \in p\,]\)
This week
\(\sem{\textit{must}}^{w} = \lambda f.\,\lambda p.\;\bigcap f(w) \subseteq p\)
\(\sem{\textit{might}}^{w} = \lambda f.\,\lambda p.\;\bigcap f(w) \cap p \neq \varnothing\)
Portner’s gloss. must p: \(f(w)\) entails \(p\). might p: \(f(w)\) is compatible with \(p\).
In view of \(\alpha\). \(\sem{\text{in view of } \alpha,\ S}^{w,f} = \sem{S}^{w,\sem{\alpha}}\): in (13) and (14) the phrase sets \(f\).

Formal

The coffee cup

Portner (2009, §3.1.3)
(31)In his office, coffee in hand, with a student standing outside the doorI must not throw my coffee cup at the student.
(32)I must throw my coffee cup at the student.
No perfect worlds. Wrongs are already done, so no world compatible with the facts obeys every moral principle.
Principles in \(f\). Then \(\bigcap f(w) = \varnothing\), and (31) and (32) are both true.
Yet a world where the cup is thrown is clearly worse. As with Chisholm, the worlds need to be ranked.
Formal

Ordering source

Ordering source. A second conversational background \(g\). \(g(w)\) is the set of ideals at \(w\), such as laws, goals or expectations.
\(u \leq_{g(w)} v\) iff every ideal in \(g(w)\) true at \(v\) is also true at \(u\). \(u\) is at least as good as \(v\).
In the figure \(g(w)\) holds three house rules: \(p\), no shoes indoors; \(q\), dishes done; \(r\), quiet after eleven. Arrows point to better worlds. \(pq\) and \(qr\) are incomparable, so the order is partial.
Formal

Best worlds

\(\mathrm{Best}(f(w), g(w)) = \{\,v \in \bigcap f(w) : \neg\exists u \in \bigcap f(w)\;\, u <_{g(w)} v\,\}\)
\(\sem{\textit{must}\ p}^{w} = 1\) iff \(\mathrm{Best}(f(w), g(w)) \subseteq p\)
\(\sem{\textit{might}\ p}^{w} = 1\) iff \(\mathrm{Best}(f(w), g(w)) \cap p \neq \varnothing\)
The coffee cup. \(f\): the circumstances. \(g\): the moral principles. Best worlds: no cup thrown. (31) true, (32) false.
Empty \(g\). No world is better than any other, so Best = \(\bigcap f(w)\), and we are back to the week 2 system.
Limit assumption. There always are best worlds. Kratzer (1981) does without it.
Formal

Solving Chisholm’s paradox

\(f\): the relevant circumstances. \(g(w)\): Mary does not rob John · robbers are punished · non-robbers are not.
(24) \(f\) leaves the robbery open. Best worlds: no robbery. True.
(26) If adds Mary robs John to \(f\). Best of those: she is punished. True.
(27) If adds Mary does not rob John to \(f\). Best of those: no punishment. True.
\(\Box q\) and \(\Box\neg q\) are true relative to different modal bases, so they do not contradict each other. Conditionals: week 8.

T and the ordering source

Deontic must. The circumstantial base is realistic, so \(w \in \bigcap f(w)\). But \(w\) need not be among the best worlds. So T fails because of the ordering source.
Epistemic must. Realistic base plus stereotypical ordering. The best worlds can leave out \(w\), so must p can be true while \(p\) is false. Kratzer’s account of the weakness in (23), and of Portner’s doctor: You must have the flu.
von Fintel & Gillies (2010). Keep T, and make indirect evidence part of the meaning of must.

Typological

Epistemic mustn’t

(41)He mustn’t be Scottish.\(\Box\neg p\)
(42)He can’t be Scottish.\(\neg\Diamond p\)
Duality. Over the same best worlds, (41) and (42) have the same truth conditions.
The standard claim. English has no epistemic mustn’t; can’t fills the gap.
Zamorano-Mansilla (2023). Epistemic mustn’t occurs in Present-day English: negative inference, like positive must. Can’t: a judgement of impossibility, usually with evidence given.
