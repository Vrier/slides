Truth, presupposition and complements

  1. (1)Anna knows that Ben called.
  2. (2)Anna doesn’t know that Ben called.
  3. (3)Anna thinks that Ben called.

(1) and (2) both convey that Ben called; (3) does not. Verbs like know take the truth of their complement for granted, even under negation.

Definitions
EntailmentS entails S′ iff S′ is true at every world at which S is true. (1) entails Ben called; (3) does not.
Veridicalx V that p entails p. Know, discover and prove are veridical; think and suspect are not.
PresuppositionA proposition that a sentence takes for granted, as part of the background the speaker and hearer already share. Mary stopped smoking presupposes that Mary used to smoke.
AssertionWhat a sentence puts forward as new information. Mary stopped smoking asserts that she does not smoke now.
TriggerThe expression that brings a presupposition in, such as stop, the possessive Mary’s or too.
ProjectionA presupposition survives when its sentence is negated, questioned or put in the antecedent of a conditional: Mary didn’t stop smoking and Did Mary stop smoking? still take for granted that Mary used to smoke.
Factivex V that p presupposes p, as (1) and (2) do. Know, discover, forget and regret are factive.
Soft triggerA trigger that loses its presupposition easily in context, as cognitive factives such as discover do. Hard triggers such as too keep it.
Declarative complementA clause introduced by that, which on its own would make a statement: Anna knows that Ben called.
Interrogative complementA clause introduced by whether, if or a wh-word, which on its own would ask a question: Anna knows whether Ben called.
Responsive, rogative, anti-rogativeResponsive verbs such as know take both kinds of complement; rogative verbs such as wonder take only interrogatives; anti-rogative verbs such as believe take only declaratives.
Neg-raisingx doesn’t V that p is understood as x Vs that not p. Think, believe, expect and want neg-raise; claim and know do not.
Strict NPIA negative polarity item that needs a negation in its own clause, such as punctual until: He didn’t arrive until Friday is fine and *He arrived until Friday is not.

Factives and neg-raising

Definitions
Factive and non-factiveKiparsky & Kiparsky’s division of clause-taking predicates. Factives such as regret take the fact that and gerunds; non-factives such as believe allow I believe Mary to be the best candidate.
SemifactiveKarttunen’s term for verbs like discover and realise, which lose their factive inference in questions and conditionals.
HoleAn operator that lets presuppositions through, such as negation.
PlugAn operator that blocks presuppositions, such as say.
FilterAn operator that lets presuppositions through only under conditions, such as if.

Presupposition, questions and the excluded middle

  1. (13)Mary’s brother is tall.

If Mary has no brother, (13) is neither true nor false. A presupposition can be modelled as a condition on when a sentence has a truth value.

Definitions
Definednessλx: φ. ψ is defined only for arguments that satisfy φ, and then returns ψ. A sentence with no truth value is marked #.
not⟦not S⟧w is defined iff ⟦S⟧w is; then 1 iff ⟦S⟧w = 0. So (2) is defined only if Ben called.
DoxDoxx(w): the worlds compatible with what x believes in w. If Anna is sure that it’s raining, it rains in every world in DoxAnna(w).
think⟦think⟧w = λp. λx. Doxx(w) ⊆ p
x thinks that p: p holds in every belief world of x.
know⟦know⟧w = λp. λx: p(w) = 1. Doxx(w) ⊆ p
Defined only if p is true at w; then true iff p holds in every belief world of x.
Example (13)
  1. ⟦Mary’s brother is tall⟧w is defined only if Mary has a brother in w.
  2. If defined, it is 1 iff Mary’s brother is tall in w.
  3. At a world where Mary has no brother it has no truth value: #.
Example (1)
  1. ⟦that Ben called⟧w = p, where p = {w′ : Ben called in w′}.
  2. ⟦knows⟧w applied to p: λx: p(w) = 1. Doxx(w) ⊆ p.
  3. Applied to Anna: defined only if Ben called in w; then 1 iff Ben called in every world in DoxAnna(w).
  4. Under not, (2) keeps the condition: defined only if Ben called; then 1 iff not every world in DoxAnna(w) is one where Ben called.
  1. (14)Alice knows that Mary left.
  2. (15)Alice knows who left.
  3. (16)Alice wonders who left.

Know takes a declarative complement in (14) and an interrogative one in (15); wonder takes only an interrogative. To give know one entry for both, a question needs a meaning that know can combine with.

Definitions
Hamblin denotationA question denotes the set of its possible answers. ⟦whether Ben called⟧ = {p, ¬p}, with p = that Ben called. ⟦who called⟧ = {that Ben called, that Cara called}.
True answerA possible answer that is true at w.
know + polar questionx knows whether p is true at w iff x knows the true answer at w. If Ben called, Anna knows whether Ben called iff she knows that Ben called.
know + wh-questionx knows who left is true at w iff x knows every true answer to who left: for each person who left, x knows that they left.

  1. (17)I don’t think it’ll rain.

(17) is understood as I think it won’t rain, although its negation sits in the higher clause. It is also the weaker sentence: someone with no opinion on the weather doesn’t think it’ll rain either.

Definitions
Excluded middleThe presupposition that the holder has an opinion on the complement: Doxx(w) ⊆ p ∨ Doxx(w) ⊆ ¬p. Anna either thinks it will rain or thinks it won’t.
think⟦think⟧w = λp. λx: Doxx(w) ⊆ p ∨ Doxx(w) ⊆ ¬p. Doxx(w) ⊆ p
Defined only if x has an opinion on p; then true iff x thinks p.
TrivialTrue whenever it has a truth value, so it tells the hearer nothing.
Belief in a questionx believes Q is true iff x believes some member of Q. Anna believes whether Ben called would be true iff she believes that he called or believes that he didn’t.

The modifier tradition

  1. (20)Chloé believes that it’s raining.
  2. (21)Chloé believes the rumour.
  3. (22)Chloé believes Deniz.
  4. (23)the rumour that it’s raining
  5. (24)*the book that it’s raining

On the Hintikka entry believe needs a proposition, so (21) and (22) do not compose as they stand. A rumour has a propositional content and a book in this sense does not, so (23) is fine and (24) is out.

  1. (25)Anna devoured the cake.
  2. (26)*Anna devoured.
  3. (27)Anna saw a red ball.
  4. (28)Anna saw a ball.
Definitions
Content nounA noun for something with a propositional content, such as rumour, claim, fact or belief. Content nouns combine with a clause; book and table do not.
ArgumentAn expression that fills a slot the head requires. Devour needs an object, so (26) is out. A function applied to its argument is saturated: the slot is used up.
ModifierAn expression that narrows down a predicate and can be left out. (27) entails (28).
Contentful individualAn individual, including an event, with a propositional content: rumours, facts, believings, explainings. content(x) is that proposition.
That-clause⟦that S⟧ = {x : content(x) = ⟦S⟧}. A predicate of contentful individuals: ⟦that it’s raining⟧ is the set of rumours, beliefs, claims and the like whose content is that it’s raining.
Event semanticsA verb is a predicate of events, of type v; its participants enter through roles: agent(e), the doer; holder(e), the one in a state, such as a believer; theme(e), what the event acts on or is about. An existential quantifier closes off the event. Angela explained the result: there is an event e with explain(e), agent(e) = Angela and theme(e) = the result.
Predicate ModificationTwo sister predicates of the same type combine by intersection: ⟦red ball⟧ = λx. red(x) ∧ ball(x), the things that are red and are balls.
ιιy[φ]: the unique y such that φ.
Example (23)
  1. ⟦rumour⟧ = λx. rumour(x)
  2. ⟦that it’s raining⟧ = λx. content(x) = p, where p = {w : it rains in w}
  3. Predicate Modification: ⟦rumour that it’s raining⟧ = λx. rumour(x) ∧ content(x) = p
  4. ⟦the rumour that it’s raining⟧ = ιx[rumour(x) ∧ content(x) = p], defined only if there is exactly one such rumour
Example (20)
  1. ⟦believes⟧ = λe. believe(e)
  2. ⟦that it’s raining⟧ = λe. content(e) = p
  3. Predicate Modification: ⟦believes that it’s raining⟧ = λe. believe(e) ∧ content(e) = p
  4. With the subject as holder and the event closed off: ∃e[believe(e) ∧ holder(e) = Chloé ∧ content(e) = p]
