Session 1 · start here

Two sentences

Session 1 · specimen one

Scalar implicature

The Horn scale \(\langle\) some, all \(\rangle\) is ordered by entailment: all entails some.
A Gricean reasoner:
1 · Observation
The speaker said some.
2 · Premise
A cooperative speaker obeys Quantity.
3 · So
She is not in a position to assert all.
4 · Premise, quiet
She knows whether all holds.
5 · Conclusion
Not all of the students passed.
Cancellable
Some of the students passed. In fact I have no idea how many. No contradiction.
Session 1 · specimen two

Presupposition

Russell 1905
The king of France is bald is false. The sentence asserts an existence claim, and that claim fails.
Strawson 1950
It is neither true nor false. The existence claim is a precondition on the sentence having a truth value at all.
Plain
Alex stopped smoking.
Negated
Alex didn't stop smoking.
Questioned
Did Alex stop smoking?
Embedded
If Alex stopped smoking, he'll feel better.
Session 1 · the inheritance

Deductive reasoning

Both specimens were built from logical machinery: an entailment scale on one side, a truth-value gap on the other. It is worth being explicit about what that machinery is.
An argument
Premises, a conclusion, and a rule licensing every step in between. Write \(\Gamma \vdash A\) for: the conclusion \(A\) is derivable from the set of premises \(\Gamma\).
Validity
If every premise is true, the conclusion cannot be false.
A question
A question we might ask is: is natural language behaviour in context really like this? Is it all clean deductive logical inference?
Session 1 · the system

A natural deduction system

Every connective gets a rule for building it in, and a rule for taking it out. Nothing else is permitted.
\(\wedge\) introduction
From \(A\) and \(B\), infer \(A \wedge B\).
\(\wedge\) elimination
From \(A \wedge B\), infer \(A\). Or infer \(B\).
\(\rightarrow\) elimination
From \(A\) and \(A \rightarrow B\), infer \(B\).
\(\rightarrow\) introduction
Assume \(A\); derive \(B\); discharge the assumption and conclude \(A \rightarrow B\).
\(\neg\) introduction
Assume \(A\); derive \(\bot\); conclude \(\neg A\).

Session 1 · the property that matters

Monotonicity

\[\text{If } \Gamma \vdash A, \text{ then } \Gamma \cup \{B\} \vdash A.\]
In words
Adding a premise never takes a conclusion away. Learn something new and everything you had already proved stays proved.
Session 1 · the test

The derivation fails both tests

Put Grice's route from some to not all through the standard just set.
Validity fails
Every premise can hold with the conclusion false. The speaker is cooperative, obeys Quantity, and says some because she has seen only two of the three students. All three passed. Premises true, conclusion false.
Monotonicity fails
Add one premise: the speaker has no idea how many passed. The conclusion disappears. No deductive system permits that.
Grice
Grice gave two criteria for implicature. Calculability: the inference can be reconstructed as an argument. Cancellability: the reconstruction can be withdrawn.
Session 2 · the strain

Where the logical picture strains

Gradience
Implicature rates vary continuously with context, and vary again across scales. A categorical rule predicts a rate of nought or one. The next slide shows what the data actually look like.
Defeasibility
Last hour's result. The first repair was attempted from inside logic: non-monotonic and default logics simply drop the rule that adding a premise preserves a conclusion. It is a real field, and it did not deliver a working pragmatics.
Epicycles
Step 4 on the derivation slide. It exists to make the argument go through, and it is stipulated rather than derived.
Noise
Real signals are corrupted and comprehension is robust to it. A logic of the literal string has nothing to say about that.
Session 2 · the strain, measured

Gradience, measured

There are ten circles. I tell you that some of the circles are white. How many are white?
What people answer
Most say four or five. And two strikes people as less likely than six. Nobody has any prior knowledge about the colouring of ten circles, so the estimate is coming from the interpretation itself.
What the categorical account predicts
Some rules out none. The implicature rules out all. Everything in between is equally good. A flat line across the whole middle of the range.
What the ratings look like
Acceptability ratings for Some of the As are Bs rise, peak around a third to a half of the set, then fall away long before the top. Two independent experiments, the same shape (van Tiel; Degen and Tanenhaus).
And scales differ from each other
some/all and sometimes/always yield the inference readily. big/enormous and attractive/stunning much less so. A theory built on entailment alone predicts no such difference (Doran et al.; van Tiel et al.).
Session 2 · the epicycle, close up

The standard recipe

1
The speaker used some.
2
One reason she did not use all: she does not know whether it holds.
3 · Competence Assumption
She knows whether it is all or some but not all.
4
So she knows the all sentence is false.
5
If she knows it, it is true.
Without step 3
You get only the weak inference: the speaker does not know that all is true. Something has to be stipulated to get the strong one.
The alternative
Treat competence as a quantity the listener is uncertain about, and infers. Week 5 does exactly this, and the stipulation disappears.
Session 2 · the move

Degrees of belief

The swap
Truth values give way to probabilities. Derivation gives way to update. A hearer holds a distribution over what the speaker might mean, and an utterance reshapes it.
What that buys
Gradient predictions for gradient data. Defeasibility for free, since new evidence shifts a posterior and nothing contradicts. Speaker uncertainty becomes representable instead of stipulated.
The logic is contained, not discarded
In week 5 you will prove a version of this: with flat priors, the probabilistic speaker prefers exactly the logically stronger message. The old picture is the special case.
Not only pragmatics
The same turn happened in artificial intelligence after Pearl, in the psychology of reasoning after Oaksford and Chater, and in vision from Helmholtz onward.
Session 2 · the working definition

What a model is

States
What the world could be. Three apples, of which some number are red.
Alternatives
What the speaker could have said. all, some, none. Change this set and the predictions change.
Priors and utilities
What was already likely, and what the speaker is trying to achieve. Weeks 4 and 2 respectively.
And a prediction that can be wrong
This is the part that matters. A model that accommodates any result predicts nothing. Every model you build this term has to say what would embarrass it.
Session 2 · where this course sits

Levels of explanation

Constraints
In a disjunction A or B, A may not entail B.  what?
Principles
Prefer the strongest reading of an ambiguous sentence.  what?
Maxims
Quantity: maximise the flow of relevant information.  what and why?
Reasons
Optimal language use: be rational, or try to be.  why?
Processes
Compute truth conditions, then apply pragmatics.  how?
This course works at the level of reasons
Derive the maxims and constraints from something more general rather than stipulating them. Anderson, on rational analysis: it "does not imply any actual logical deduction in choosing optimal behavior, only that the behavior will be optimized".
The other levels stay
A constraint can be correct as a description while a model at the level of reasons explains why it holds. Franke and Jäger treat these as different tools aimed at the same task. Processes, the bottom row, answer a separate question: week 6.
