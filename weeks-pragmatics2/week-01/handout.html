Implicature and Presupposition

0

Speaker meaning and the Cooperative Principle

Definitions
Sentence meaningThe conventional contribution of the expressions.
Speaker meaningWhat the speaker communicates by choosing them here.
What is saidThe contextually fixed content expressed.
What is implicatedAdditional inferred content.
Conversational implicatureWhat is communicated beyond what is said, derived from assumptions about rational, cooperative interaction.
EntailmentA entails B when B holds in every situation in which A holds. Entailments cannot be cancelled and are monotonic.
Cooperative PrincipleAssume participants are making appropriate contributions to the purpose of the exchange.
QualityTry to make your contribution true.
QuantityBe as informative as required.
RelationBe relevant.
MannerBe perspicuous.
  1. (1)A: Are you coming to the meeting?   B: I have marking to do.

1

Implicature by maxim

Context. Each maxim generates a characteristic kind of inference. Quality implicatures often involve apparent falsity treated as purposeful. Quantity implicatures arise when a weaker statement is used in place of a stronger one. Relation implicatures arise when relevance has to be reconstructed. Manner implicatures arise when a speaker is unexpectedly indirect, long, or opaque.

Definitions
FloutingA maxim is violated at the level of what is said and observed at the level of what is meant.
Generalised implicatureCarried by the form itself, absent a special context.
Particularised implicatureCarried only in a particular context, as in (1).
  1. (2)[someone arrives drenched]   Lovely weather.
  2. (3)[a colleague submits a clearly unfinished draft]   This is extremely polished.
  3. (4)[a student ignores every instruction]   You followed the brief perfectly.
  4. (5)Pat read the abstract.
  5. (6)Pat produced a sequence of sounds closely corresponding to the score.

Exercise.

(a)For each of (2) to (6), state the implicature and the maxim flouted.
(b)In (2) to (4) the speaker says something they believe false. Say why this does not make them uncooperative.

2

Diagnostics for Implicature

Definitions
MonotonicAdding further premises never withdraws a conclusion: if Γ ⊢ A then Γ ∪ {B} ⊢ A.
Defeasibility / non-monotonicityAn inference is defeasible when later material can withdraw it.
Diagnostics
  1. Cancellability. The inference can be cancelled without contradiction.
  2. Reinforceability. The inference can be made explicit without redundancy.
  3. Calculability. It is possible to argue from context plus maxims to the inference.
  1. (7)Some of the students passed. In fact I have no idea how many.
  2. (8)Some, but not all, of the students passed.
  3. (9)Some of the students passed. In fact none of them did.

Exercise.

(a)(7) is fine and (9) is not. Say precisely what the difference shows.
(b)(8) is not redundant. Say what follows about whether not all is part of what is said.
(c)Run all three diagnostics on the implicature of (5). Report any that fail.
3

Scalar implicature and strengthening

Context. Scalar implicature is a Quantity-style inference driven by alternatives, and it is a strengthening phenomenon: what is communicated is logically stronger than what is said. A speaker uses a weaker term on a scale; the hearer considers stronger alternatives; the strengthened interpretation excludes at least some of them.

Definitions
ScaleA set of alternative expressions ordered by logical strength.
AlternativeAn expression the speaker could have used in place of the one uttered.
Logical strengthA statement A is logically stronger than a statement B iff A entails B but B does not entail A.
DomainScales
Quantifiersall > most > many > some
Modalsmust > should > may
Numeralsthree > two > one
Connectivesand > or
Adverbsalways > often > sometimes
A Gricean derivation template
  1. Utterance: Some of the students passed.
  2. Stronger alternative: All of the students passed.
  3. Quantity pressure: the stronger would be more informative.
  4. Quality pressure: do not assert what you lack evidence for.
  5. Inference: the speaker is not in a position to assert the stronger alternative.
  6. Typical strengthening: not all.

Exercise.

(a)Run the template on one item from each row of the table. Give the utterance, the alternative, and the strengthening.

4

Where alternatives come from

Sources of ordering
  1. Entailment. All entails some.
  2. Context. Destinations, rankings, plans, routes.
  3. Discourse. What would count as a stronger answer to the current question.

Not every scale is ordered by entailment. Travel and progress scales are ordered relative to a goal. Context: travelling north from Cork to Belfast, on the scale ⟨Cork, Wexford, Dublin, Belfast⟩.

  1. (10)I made it to Wexford.
  2. (11)I have written to every headmaster in Rochdale.

Fox & Katzir 2011. On the characterization of alternatives. Natural Language Semantics 19: 87–107.

5

Presupposition

Definitions
PresuppositionContent the utterance requires to be already in place, rather than content it puts forward.
AssertionThe content the speaker puts forward as a contribution to the conversation.
Presupposed contentContent treated as background, and preserved where asserted content is reversed or destroyed.
ProjectionA presupposition survives embedding under an operator that removes the assertion.
TriggerA lexical item or construction that introduces a presupposition.
Common groundThe propositions interlocutors treat as mutually accepted for current purposes.
AccommodationThe hearer’s silent repair of the context so that the utterance becomes acceptable.
  1. (12)The prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.

Asserted: the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin. Presupposed: Switzerland has a prime minister.

Projection under embedding
  1. Negation. The prime minister of Switzerland is not visiting Dublin. Asserted content reversed; presupposed content preserved.
  2. Question. Is the prime minister of Switzerland visiting Dublin? Asserted content destroyed; presupposed content preserved.
  3. Conditional antecedent. If the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, the meeting will be rescheduled. Asserted content destroyed; presupposed content preserved.

Hey wait a minute. Background requirements can be challenged in a way asserted content cannot.

  1. (13)The prime minister of Burkina Faso is visiting Dublin.

Two replies: No … he isn't visiting. And: Hey, wait a minute … Burkina Faso doesn't have a prime minister.

7

Holes, plugs and filters

Definitions
The projection problemHow the presuppositions of a complex sentence are determined by those of its parts and by the environments embedding them.
Local supportEarlier material in the sentence already entails the presupposition, so nothing needs to project.
Three environments
  1. Holes let presuppositions project. The prime minister of Switzerland is not visiting Dublin.
  2. Plugs can block projection to the common ground by confining the presupposition to an embedded context. Maria thinks that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  3. Filters make projection depend on other material in the sentence. If Switzerland has a prime minister, the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.

Exercise. For each item: identify a presupposition candidate; classify the environment as hole, plug or filter; justify in one or two sentences by appealing to projection behaviour.

  1. (24)Jamie knows that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  2. (25)Jamie believes that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  3. (26)A journalist said that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  4. (27)Maybe the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  5. (28)Either Switzerland has no prime minister, or the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  6. (29)Either the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, or the meeting will be cancelled.
  7. (30)Alex thinks that China has stopped stockpiling metals.

Filtering practice. For each pair, decide whether the presupposition projects from the whole sentence, and justify. Order matters.

(a)Switzerland has a prime minister, and the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.  /  The prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, and Switzerland has a prime minister.
(b)If Switzerland has a prime minister, the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.  /  If the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, Switzerland has a prime minister.
