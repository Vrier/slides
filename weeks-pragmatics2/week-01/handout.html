Implicature and Presupposition

0

Speaker meaning and the Cooperative Principle

Context. Pragmatics is meaning in context, derived by inference. Two distinctions organise the field.

Sentence meaning is the conventional contribution of the expressions. Speaker meaning is what the speaker communicates by choosing them here. What is said is the contextually fixed content expressed. What is implicated is additional inferred content.

The Cooperative Principle
  1. Assume participants are making appropriate contributions to the purpose of the exchange.
  2. Quality. Try to make your contribution true.
  3. Quantity. Be as informative as required.
  4. Relation. Be relevant.
  5. Manner. Be perspicuous.
  1. (1)A: Are you coming to the meeting?   B: I have marking to do.

Exercise.

(a)For (1), separate what B said from what B implicated.
(b)Name the maxim that licenses the inference, and state the reasoning in one sentence.

Grice 1975. Logic and conversation. In Syntax and Semantics 3.

The maxims are not rules speakers obey. They are assumptions hearers make, and the inference comes from applying them to a choice the speaker actually made.

1

Implicature by maxim

Context. Each maxim generates a characteristic kind of inference. Quality implicatures often involve apparent falsity treated as purposeful. Quantity implicatures arise when a weaker statement is used in place of a stronger one. Relation implicatures arise when relevance has to be reconstructed. Manner implicatures arise when a speaker is unexpectedly indirect, long, or opaque.

  1. (2)[someone arrives drenched]   Lovely weather.
  2. (3)[a colleague submits a clearly unfinished draft]   This is extremely polished.
  3. (4)[a student ignores every instruction]   You followed the brief perfectly.
  4. (5)Pat read the abstract.
  5. (6)Pat produced a sequence of sounds closely corresponding to the score.

Exercise.

(a)For each of (2) to (6), state the implicature and name the maxim at work.
(b)In (2) to (4) the speaker says something they believe false. Say why this does not make them uncooperative.
(c)Rewrite (6) in the obvious short way. Say what is lost, and what that shows about Manner.

Grice 1975. On flouting: the maxim is violated at the level of what is said, and observed at the level of what is meant.

2

Three diagnostics

Context. Implicature is distinguished from entailment and from conventional meaning by how it behaves under three tests.

Diagnostics
  1. Cancellability. The inference can be cancelled without contradiction.
  2. Reinforceability. The inference can be made explicit without redundancy.
  3. Calculability. It is possible to argue from context plus maxims to the inference.
  1. (7)Some of the students passed. In fact I have no idea how many.
  2. (8)Some, but not all, of the students passed.
  3. (9)Some of the students passed. In fact none of them did.

Exercise.

(a)(7) is fine and (9) is not. Say precisely what the difference shows.
(b)(8) is not redundant. Say what follows about whether not all is part of what is said.
(c)Run all three diagnostics on the implicature of (5). Report any that fail.

Cancellability is the load-bearing one. An entailment cannot be cancelled; an implicature can. Keep the contrast in (7) and (9) in view for the rest of the term.

3

Scalar implicature and strengthening

Context. Scalar implicature is a Quantity-style inference driven by alternatives, and it is a strengthening phenomenon: what is communicated is logically stronger than what is said. A speaker uses a weaker term on a scale; the hearer considers stronger alternatives; the strengthened interpretation excludes at least some of them.

DomainScale, strongest first
Quantifiersall > most > many > some
Modalsmust > should > may
Numeralsthree > two > one
Connectivesand > or
Adverbsalways > often > sometimes
A Gricean derivation template
  1. Utterance: Some of the students passed.
  2. Stronger alternative: All of the students passed.
  3. Quantity pressure: the stronger would be more informative.
  4. Quality pressure: do not assert what you lack evidence for.
  5. Inference: the speaker is not in a position to assert the stronger alternative.
  6. Typical strengthening: not all.

Exercise.

(a)Run the template on one item from each row of the table. Give the utterance, the alternative, and the strengthening.
(b)Step 5 delivers something weaker than step 6. State the gap, and name the extra assumption that closes it.

Horn 1972. On the semantic properties of logical operators in English. The original scales.

Step 5 to step 6 is the epistemic step. Without it you get only that the speaker does not know the stronger alternative to hold.

4

Where alternatives come from

Context. Scalar inferences depend on how alternatives are ordered, and the ordering has more than one source.

Sources of ordering
  1. Entailment. All entails some.
  2. Context. Destinations, rankings, plans, routes.
  3. Discourse. What would count as a stronger answer to the current question.

Not every scale is ordered by entailment. Travel and progress scales are ordered relative to a goal. Context: travelling north from Cork to Belfast, on the scale ⟨Cork, Wexford, Dublin, Belfast⟩.

  1. (10)I made it to Wexford.
  2. (11)I have written to every headmaster in Rochdale.

Exercise.

(a)Give the implicature of (10) and say which source of ordering supplies it. Show that entailment does not.
(b)Construct a context in which (10) carries no such implicature. Say what changed.
(c)The first analytic step is always: what are the relevant alternatives here? Answer it for (11).

Ordering by context and by discourse will matter later: both are places where a probabilistic model has something to say and an entailment scale does not.

5

Two pressure points

Context. A purely pragmatic account treats implicature as a global inference from an utterance, computed after composition. Two patterns strain that picture.

Embedding. Strengthened meanings can arise in embedded positions, interacting with other operators.

  1. (12)Everyone who solved some of the problems passed.

Symmetry. Naive exclusion overgenerates if alternatives are treated symmetrically.

  1. (13)John or Mary came.

Plausible alternatives for (13): John came, Mary came, John and Mary came. The typical strengthening is not both.

Exercise.

(a)Give the embedded reading of (12). Say why a global account has trouble producing it.
(b)Negate each alternative to (13) in turn. Show that negating all of them contradicts the utterance.
(c)State the constraint a working account needs in order to exclude some alternatives and not others.

Katzir 2007. Structurally-defined alternatives. Linguistics and Philosophy 30: 669–690.

One repair: exclude only those alternatives that can be jointly negated while keeping the prejacent true.

6

Exhaustification

Context. A different idea: treat at least some scalar implicatures as the output of a grammatical operator. An exhaustivity operator EXH applies to a proposition and strengthens it relative to alternatives, and it can apply locally inside larger structures.

EXH in one line
  1. EXH(p) asserts p and excludes certain alternatives to p.
  2. You need a set of alternatives Alt(p), and a criterion for which may be excluded.

Only is an overt exhaustifier. A standard move is to treat EXH as its covert analogue.

  1. (14)Only Mary came.
  2. (15)Did Mary win the race?

A standard route to alternatives is focus. Stress MARY, WIN or RACE in (15) and the alternative set changes: {Mary won, Alex won, …}, {Mary won, Mary placed, Mary finished, …}, {Mary won the race, Mary won the marathon, …}.

Exercise.

(a)Give what (14) conveys, and write it in the EXH schema.
(b)Take the question Who came? and the answer Mary. Model the exhaustive interpretation: give p, give Alt(p), apply EXH.
(c)Show how EXH applying locally delivers the embedded reading of (12).
(d)Scalar enrichment is now part of compositional meaning. Say what is gained and what is given up.

Fox 2007. Free choice and the theory of scalar implicatures.

Chierchia 2013. Logic in Grammar. Oxford University Press.

Presupposition: asserted and presupposed content

Context. Some content is asserted; some is required as background for the utterance to be appropriate at all.

  1. (16)The prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.

Asserted: the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin. Presupposed: Switzerland has a prime minister.

Projection under embedding
  1. Negation. The prime minister of Switzerland is not visiting Dublin. Asserted content reversed; presupposed content preserved.
  2. Question. Is the prime minister of Switzerland visiting Dublin? Asserted content destroyed; presupposed content preserved.
  3. Conditional antecedent. If the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, the meeting will be rescheduled. Asserted content destroyed; presupposed content preserved.

Hey wait a minute. Background requirements can be challenged in a way asserted content cannot.

  1. (17)The prime minister of Burkina Faso is visiting Dublin.

Two replies: No … he isn't visiting. And: Hey, wait a minute … Burkina Faso doesn't have a prime minister.

Exercise.

(a)Say which reply to (17) targets asserted content and which targets presupposed content.
(b)Apply the cancellation test from block 2 to the presupposition of (16). Record what goes wrong.

Strawson 1950. On referring.

The Hey wait a minute test is the sharpest informal diagnostic: it isolates content the speaker treated as already settled.

Triggers

9

Holes, plugs and filters

Context. Embedding environments differ in what they do to a presupposition.

Three environments
  1. Holes let presuppositions project. The prime minister of Switzerland is not visiting Dublin.
  2. Plugs can block projection to the common ground by confining the presupposition to an embedded context. Maria thinks that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  3. Filters make projection depend on other material in the sentence. If Switzerland has a prime minister, the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.

Exercise. For each item: identify a presupposition candidate; classify the environment as hole, plug or filter; justify in one or two sentences by appealing to projection behaviour.

  1. (28)Jamie knows that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  2. (29)Jamie believes that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  3. (30)A journalist said that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  4. (31)Maybe the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  5. (32)Either Switzerland has no prime minister, or the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
  6. (33)Either the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, or the meeting will be cancelled.
  7. (34)Alex thinks that China has stopped stockpiling metals.

Filtering practice. For each pair, decide whether the presupposition projects from the whole sentence, and justify. Order matters.

(a)Switzerland has a prime minister, and the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.  /  The prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, and Switzerland has a prime minister.
(b)If Switzerland has a prime minister, the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.  /  If the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin, Switzerland has a prime minister.

Karttunen 1973. Presuppositions of compound sentences. Linguistic Inquiry 4.

The asymmetry in the conjunction pair is the classic argument that projection is computed incrementally, left to right.

10

Gradedness

Context. Blocks 7 to 9 treat projection as categorical: content projects or it does not, and an environment is a hole or it is not. The experimental record does not support that.

Two findings
  1. Projection is gradient, and tracks at-issueness. Across a large set of expressions, content projects to the extent that it is not at-issue. The correlation between not-at-issueness and projectivity is very high.
  2. Prior beliefs modulate projection. The more plausible the content is independently, the more the speaker is taken to be committed to it. Projection is therefore sensitive to world knowledge, not only to the trigger.

A further result: the factive class is heterogeneous, and there is little empirical support for a categorical split between factive and non-factive predicates.

Exercise.

(a)Take (18), (19) and (29). Rank the three by how strongly the presupposition survives. Say what your ranking tracks.
(b)Construct two versions of Alex discovered that the test was hard: one where the complement is independently plausible, one where it is not. Judge projection in each.
(c)A categorical hole-plug-filter account predicts no difference in (b). State what a theory would need in order to predict one.

Degen & Tonhauser 2021. Prior beliefs modulate projection. Open Mind 5: 59–70.
