Presentation Topics

1

Hyperbole, irony and metaphor

Speakers often mean something other than the literal content. The kettle cost a million pounds: the kettle was expensive, and the speaker is annoyed. What lovely weather, said in a downpour: the opposite of the literal content. RSA models treat these as inference about the question under discussion, the aspect of the world the speaker is talking about. The listener infers the question and the meaning together.

Questions
  1. What does the listener infer beyond the literal content in hyperbole, in irony, and in metaphor?
  2. How much of the work does the question under discussion do, and where does the set of possible questions come from?
  3. What would a Relevance Theory account of the same examples look like, and what data would separate the two?
Readings

Kao, J. T., Bergen, L. & Goodman, N. D. (2014). Formalizing the pragmatics of metaphor understanding. In Proceedings of the 36th Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society.

Kao, J. T. & Goodman, N. D. (2015). Let's talk (ironically) about the weather: Modeling verbal irony. In Proceedings of the 37th Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society.

Kao, J. T., Wu, J. Y., Bergen, L. & Goodman, N. D. (2014). Nonliteral understanding of number words. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 111(33), 12002–12007.

Scontras, G., Tessler, M. H. & Franke, M. Probabilistic Language Understanding: An Introduction to the Rational Speech Act Framework, chapter 3. michael-franke.github.io/probLang

2

Vagueness and gradable adjectives

Whether Sam is tall is true depends on an unstated standard. Tall for a jockey is short for a basketball player. Probabilistic accounts treat the threshold as a variable the listener infers, from context and from prior knowledge of the comparison class. Two versions: listener-oriented (Lassiter & Goodman) and speaker-oriented (Qing & Franke).

Questions
  1. How does the prior over heights, prices or temperatures fix the threshold?
  2. Where do the listener-oriented and speaker-oriented models make different predictions, and for which adjectives?
  3. What do these models say about the sorites paradox, and about relative adjectives (tall) against absolute adjectives (full)?
Readings

Kennedy, C. (2007). Vagueness and grammar: The semantics of relative and absolute gradable adjectives. Linguistics and Philosophy 30(1), 1–45.

Lassiter, D. & Goodman, N. D. (2017). Adjectival vagueness in a Bayesian model of interpretation. Synthese 194(10), 3801–3836.

Qing, C. & Franke, M. (2014). Gradable adjectives, vagueness, and optimal language use: A speaker-oriented model. Semantics and Linguistic Theory 24, 23–41.

Scontras, G., Tessler, M. H. & Franke, M. Probabilistic Language Understanding, chapter 5. michael-franke.github.io/probLang

3

Politeness and social meaning

Asked about a colleague's weak talk, most speakers avoid It was bad. Brown & Levinson: speakers protect face, the public self-image of speaker and hearer. Formal accounts put social goals into the speaker's utility. The speaker trades informativeness against kindness, and against appearing kind. Related work applies signalling games to sociolinguistic variation: the choice between -in’ and -ing as a way of presenting an identity.

Questions
  1. How do social goals enter the speaker's utility, and how are their weights estimated from data?
  2. What does the formal account add to Brown & Levinson, and what does it leave out?
  3. Does the social meaning studied in sociolinguistics fit the same machinery as implicature?
Readings

Brown, P. & Levinson, S. C. (1987). Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

Burnett, H. (2019). Signalling games, sociolinguistic variation and the construction of style. Linguistics and Philosophy 42, 419–450.

Yoon, E. J., Tessler, M. H., Goodman, N. D. & Frank, M. C. (2020). Polite speech emerges from competing social goals. Open Mind 4, 71–87.

4

Reference, redundancy and convention formation

Speakers often say more than a referent requires: the small blue mug, with one mug on the table. Strict Gricean reasoning counts this as overinformative. Listeners find such descriptions easy to use, and speakers produce them systematically, colour more often than size. Over repeated reference, partners also shorten their descriptions: the one that looks like an ice skater becomes the skater. Both patterns test reference-game models.

Questions
  1. Why are some redundant modifiers more likely than others, and what does a model need to capture the asymmetry?
  2. How do partners build conventions over repeated reference? Does this need a model of learning as well as inference?
  3. How do these conversational conventions relate to Lewis's signalling games?
Readings

Clark, H. H. & Wilkes-Gibbs, D. (1986). Referring as a collaborative process. Cognition 22(1), 1–39.

Degen, J., Hawkins, R. D., Graf, C., Kreiss, E. & Goodman, N. D. (2020). When redundancy is useful: A Bayesian approach to ‘overinformative’ referring expressions. Psychological Review 127(4), 591–621.

Hawkins, R. D., Frank, M. C. & Goodman, N. D. (2020). Characterizing the dynamics of learning in repeated reference games. Cognitive Science 44(6), e12845.

5

Embedded implicatures

Gricean reasoning applies to whole utterances, and predicts implicatures at the level of the sentence. Every student read some of the books seems to have a reading with some but not all inside the scope of every. Three responses. Grammatical: a covert exhaustification operator in the syntax (Chierchia, Fox & Spector). Pragmatic: the listener reasons about uncertainty in the lexicon (Potts et al.). Hybrid: the grammar supplies candidate readings, pragmatic reasoning selects among them (Franke & Bergen).

Questions
  1. What is the evidence for embedded readings, and how robust is it experimentally?
  2. How does the exhaustification operator work, and what constrains where it appears?
  3. Can the grammatical and pragmatic accounts be separated empirically?
Readings

Chierchia, G., Fox, D. & Spector, B. (2012). Scalar implicature as a grammatical phenomenon. In C. Maienborn, K. von Heusinger & P. Portner (eds.), Semantics: An International Handbook of Natural Language Meaning, vol. 3, 2297–2331. Berlin: De Gruyter Mouton.

Franke, M. & Bergen, L. (2020). Theory-driven statistical modeling for semantics and pragmatics: A case study on grammatically generated implicature readings. Language 96(2), e77–e96.

Geurts, B. & Pouscoulous, N. (2009). Embedded implicatures?!? Semantics and Pragmatics 2(4), 1–34.

Potts, C., Lassiter, D., Levy, R. & Frank, M. C. (2016). Embedded implicatures as pragmatic inferences under compositional lexical uncertainty. Journal of Semantics 33(4), 755–802.

6

Gradient presupposition projection

The complement of know tends to be taken as true under negation and in questions. Did Kim know that Sam left? still suggests that Sam left. Classical accounts: predicates are factive or non-factive, and content projects or it does not. The experimental picture: projection comes in degrees across predicates, and is sensitive to the listener's prior beliefs about the content. No current analysis captures the full pattern.

Questions
  1. What do the gradient data show, and which classical assumptions do they challenge?
  2. How might prior beliefs enter a model of projection? How does this relate to presupposition as near-certainty in the prior?
  3. Are factive predicates still a useful category?
Readings

Degen, J. & Tonhauser, J. (2021). Prior beliefs modulate projection. Open Mind 5, 59–70.

Degen, J. & Tonhauser, J. (2022). Are there factive predicates? An empirical investigation. Language 98(3), 552–591.

Tonhauser, J., Beaver, D. I. & Degen, J. (2018). How projective is projective content? Gradience in projectivity and at-issueness. Journal of Semantics 35(3), 495–542.

7

Pragmatic development in children

Young children often accept Some of the horses jumped over the fence when all of them did. Adults reject it. The early conclusion: children cannot yet draw scalar implicatures. Later work places the difficulty elsewhere: access to alternatives, the demands of the task, or the meaning of the quantifiers. Children succeed early when the alternatives are present in the context.

Questions
  1. What do children fail at in scalar implicature tasks, and what do they succeed at?
  2. How do changes to the task (graded judgements, alternatives given in context) change the results?
  3. What does a model of pragmatic development need: a changing lexicon, changing alternatives, or changing reasoning?
Readings

Barner, D., Brooks, N. & Bale, A. (2011). Accessing the unsaid: The role of scalar alternatives in children's pragmatic inference. Cognition 118(1), 84–93.

Katsos, N. & Bishop, D. V. M. (2011). Pragmatic tolerance: Implications for the acquisition of informativeness and implicature. Cognition 120(1), 67–81.

Noveck, I. A. (2001). When children are more logical than adults: Experimental investigations of scalar implicature. Cognition 78(2), 165–188.

Stiller, A. J., Goodman, N. D. & Frank, M. C. (2015). Ad-hoc implicature in preschool children. Language Learning and Development 11(2), 176–190.

8

Relevance Theory against RSA

Relevance Theory and RSA both explain how hearers go beyond literal meaning. They describe the process differently. Relevance Theory: the hearer follows a path of least effort, and stops at the first interpretation that meets their expectation of relevance. RSA: the hearer reasons over the space of utterances and meanings, and arrives at the most probable interpretation. The two literatures rarely engage with each other. No experiment yet separates them.

Questions
  1. At what level of explanation does each theory sit: the process, its outcome, or both?
  2. What predictions does satisficing make that optimisation does not, and the reverse?
  3. What experiment could separate them?
Readings

Degen, J. (2023). The Rational Speech Act framework. Annual Review of Linguistics 9, 519–540.

Sperber, D. & Wilson, D. (1995). Relevance: Communication and Cognition, 2nd edn. Oxford: Blackwell.

Unger, C. & Buschmeier, H. (2025). Conversational implicatures: Modelling Relevance Theory probabilistically. arXiv:2509.22354.

Wilson, D. & Sperber, D. (2004). Relevance theory. In L. R. Horn & G. Ward (eds.), The Handbook of Pragmatics, 607–632. Oxford: Blackwell.

9

Language models and theory of mind

Pragmatic inference seems to require reasoning about what other people know, believe and intend. Large language models are trained only to predict text. Some pass false-belief tasks and choose pragmatic interpretations of utterances. Two views. On one, abilities like theory of mind have emerged as a by-product of learning language. On the other, the models rely on shallower patterns, which break when the tasks are altered slightly.

Questions
  1. What do false-belief tasks measure in children, and does success mean the same for a language model?
  2. How should tests be designed so that success cannot come from surface patterns in the training data?
  3. What do the results show about how much of human pragmatic competence depends on reasoning about other minds?
Readings

Hu, J., Floyd, S., Jouravlev, O., Fedorenko, E. & Gibson, E. (2023). A fine-grained comparison of pragmatic language understanding in humans and language models. In Proceedings of the 61st Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, 4194–4213.

Kosinski, M. (2024). Evaluating large language models in theory of mind tasks. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 121(45), e2405460121.

Ullman, T. (2023). Large language models fail on trivial alterations to theory-of-mind tasks. arXiv:2302.08399.
