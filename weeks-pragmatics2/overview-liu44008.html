What the module is about

Pragmatics is the study of meaning in use: how speakers choose what to say, and how hearers work out what they mean. The classical tradition, from Grice onwards, treats pragmatic inference as reasoning from premises about the speaker and the conversation. Much recent work treats it as reasoning under uncertainty. Speakers and hearers hold beliefs of different strengths, alternative utterances compete, and the inferences drawn from them come in degrees of confidence. Pragmatic theories of this kind make quantitative predictions, which can be tested against experimental data.

This module explores different frameworks informed by Game Theory, Information Theory, and Cognitive Science to approach pragmatic phenomena. In the final weeks, students apply them to a topic of their choice, from non-literal language and politeness to children's pragmatic development and language models.

Learning outcomes

On successful completion of this module, students will be able to:

  1. explain how contemporary approaches to pragmatics draw on game theory, information theory and cognitive science, and what each contributes to the study of meaning in use;
  2. apply these approaches, including Rational Speech Act models, to scalar implicature, presupposition and a wider range of phenomena such as vagueness and non-literal language, and assess where they succeed and where they run into difficulty;
  3. read and critically evaluate current research in pragmatics, including experimental and developmental studies and work on language models;
  4. situate contemporary pragmatics against the logical tradition it grew out of, and against open questions about the boundary between semantics and pragmatics;
  5. present an original analysis of a pragmatic phenomenon to an academic audience.

Presentation topics

Weeks 9 to 12 will include a student presentation on the Friday chosen from the topics below.

  1. Hyperbole, irony and metaphor. Hearers work out that a speaker didn't mean the literal content by inferring what the speaker is trying to convey: a feeling, an attitude, a salient feature. Kao, Wu, Bergen & Goodman (2014); Kao & Goodman (2015); probLang ch. 3.
  2. Vagueness and gradable adjectives. The threshold for tall is treated as a variable the listener infers from context and prior expectations. Lassiter & Goodman (2017); Qing & Franke (2014); probLang ch. 5.
  3. Politeness and social meaning. Speakers trade informativeness against kindness and self-presentation. Brown & Levinson (1987); Yoon, Tessler, Goodman & Frank (2020); Burnett (2019).
  4. Reference, redundancy and convention formation. Speakers say more than a minimal description needs, and partners shorten their descriptions over repeated reference. Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs (1986); Degen et al. (2020); Hawkins, Frank & Goodman (2020).
  5. Embedded implicatures: grammar or pragmatics? Implicatures that seem to arise inside the scope of other operators are the grammatical account's strongest evidence. Chierchia, Fox & Spector (2012); Potts, Lassiter, Levy & Frank (2016); Franke & Bergen (2020).
  6. Gradient presupposition projection. Projection comes in degrees and is sensitive to prior beliefs, and no current analysis captures the full pattern. Tonhauser, Beaver & Degen (2018); Degen & Tonhauser (2021).
  7. Pragmatic development in children. Children fail some implicature tasks and pass others, and the question is where the difficulty lies. Barner, Brooks & Bale (2011); Katsos & Bishop (2011); Stiller, Goodman & Frank (2015).
  8. Relevance Theory against RSA. Relevance Theory describes comprehension as satisficing and RSA as optimisation, and no experiment has yet been designed to tell them apart. Wilson & Sperber (2004); Degen (2023); Unger & Buschmeier (2025).
  9. Language models and theory of mind. Whether large language models track what other agents know and believe, and what their successes and failures show about the reasoning pragmatic inference relies on. Kosinski (2023); Ullman (2023); Hu et al. (2023).
