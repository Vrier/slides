Probability from counts

Definitions
Probabilitycount of an outcometotal count. A number from 0 to 1.
ComplementP(not A) = 1 − P(A).
Probability distributionA function P from outcomes to [0, 1] whose values sum to 1.
Uniform distributionEvery outcome has the same probability.
Normal distributionSymmetric bell curve with mean μ and standard deviation σ. About 0.68 of values lie within σ of μ, about 0.95 within 2σ.

Independent events

Definitions
IndependentOne happening leaves the probability of the other unchanged.
Product ruleFor independent A and B, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

Exercise.

(a)Given two rolls of a fair 6 sided die, what are: P(two sixes) and P(at least one six).
P(six) = 16 ≈ 0.17. P(two sixes) = 16 × 16 = 136 ≈ 0.028. P(at least one six) = 1 − P(no six) = 1 − 56 × 56 = 1 − 2536 ≈ 0.31.
(b)Assuming double or nothing, would you take either of these bets?
  1. Rolling a single fair die up to 4 times and winning if at least one 6 appears.
  2. Rolling a pair of fair dice up to 24 times and winning if at least one double-six appears.
A. P(at least one 6 in 4 rolls) = 1 − (56)4 ≈ 1 − 0.482 = 0.518. Above 0.5, so double or nothing wins on average. Take it.
B. P(double six on one roll) = 136. P(at least one double six in 24 rolls) = 1 − (3536)24 ≈ 1 − 0.509 = 0.491. Below 0.5, so double or nothing loses on average. Turn it down.
(c)Draw a Scrabble tile, put it back, draw again. P(two vowels)? Without putting it back, why are the two draws no longer independent?
With the tile put back, 0.42 × 0.42 ≈ 0.18. Without it, the second draw comes from 99 tiles, and how many vowels are left depends on the first draw. P(second vowel | first vowel) = 4199 ≈ 0.41, so P(two vowels) = 0.42 × 0.41 ≈ 0.17.

Pascal & Fermat 1654. Letters on the problem of points.

Definitions
Surprisallog2 1p bits. How many halvings take 1 down to p.
EntropyH = Σx P(x) · log2 1P(x). Average surprisal of a distribution.
InformationHow far an observation lowers the entropy.

Bayes’ rule

Definitions
Hypothesis HThe claim you want to know about.
Evidence EWhat you have observed.
PriorP(H), before the evidence.
LikelihoodP(E | H), how expected the evidence is if H is true.
PosteriorP(H | E), after the evidence.
Bayes’ ruleP(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H)P(E).
Evidence termP(E) = P(E | H) · P(H) + P(E | not H) · P(not H).
Conditional probabilityP(A | B) = P(A and B)P(B).
Joint probabilityP(A and B) = P(A | B) · P(B) = P(B | A) · P(A).

Exercise.

(a)1 essay in 20 is written by AI. A detector flags an AI essay with probability 0.9 and a human essay with probability 0.05. Draw the tree for 1,000 essays and find P(AI | flagged).
Of 1,000 essays, 50 are by AI and 45 of them are flagged. Of the 950 human essays, 47.5 are flagged. P(AI | flagged) = 4545 + 47.5 ≈ 0.49. By the rule, 0.9 × 0.050.9 × 0.05 + 0.05 × 0.95 ≈ 0.49.
(b)A second, independent detector flags the same essay, with the same probabilities. Use your answer to (a) as the new prior.
0.9 × 0.490.9 × 0.49 + 0.05 × 0.51 ≈ 0.440.44 + 0.026 ≈ 0.94.

Speaker’s choicesP(w | s), how often the speaker uses word w for object s. Each row sums to 1.

InformativeA word is more informative for an object the higher the listener’s posterior for that object, and the fewer bits of surprisal, log2 1P(s | w), are left.

Degrees of belief and expected value

Definitions
Expected valueΣx P(x) × value(x). The average payoff over many plays.
Degree of beliefProbability as how strongly someone believes: the most they would pay for a ticket worth €1 if it comes true.
Dutch bookA set of bets that loses whatever happens. Possible when degrees of belief break the laws of probability.
RationalDegrees of belief that obey the laws of probability.

England v France. Anna’s beliefs: France 0.55, draw 0.30, England 0.25, total 1.10. The bookmaker sells €1 tickets at €0.52, €0.28 and €0.23, each below her belief.

She buys all three: €1.03. Exactly one result happens: she gets €1. Net €1 − €1.03 = −€0.03.
