Levelling

Poker players climb a ladder of guesses about one another. They call it levelling.
First level: what hand do I have?
Second level: what hand does my opponent have?
Third level: what hand does my opponent think I have?
Fourth level: what hand does my opponent think I think he has?
Fifth level: what hand does my opponent think I think he thinks I have?

The prisoner's dilemma

Dominant strategy. An option that is a best response whatever the other player does. Here confessing beats silence against either column: \(5>3\) and \(1>0\).
Both confess and land at \((1,1)\), though \((3,3)\) was on the table. Interests conflict here.
Empirical

Nash equilibrium

Best response. \(a_i\) is a best response to the others' choices \(a_{{-i}}\) iff \(u_i(a_i,a_{{-i}})\ge u_i(a_i',a_{{-i}})\) for every alternative \(a_i'\).
Nash equilibrium. A cell in which each choice is a best response to the other: nobody gains by deviating alone. In Nash's words, “A self-countering \(n\)-tuple is called an equilibrium point.” Strict when every lone deviation strictly loses; every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies.
Empirical

Coordination games

Coordination equilibrium. Lewisian equilibria in a coordination game: a profile \(a\) such that nobody would have been better off had any one agent alone acted otherwise, \(u_i(a)\ge u_i(a')\) for every agent \(i\) and every \(a'\) differing from \(a\) in a single agent's choice. Nash “does your deviation help you”, Lewis “does anyone's deviation help anyone?”.
Coordination problem. A situation of interdependent decision in which coincidence of interest predominates and which has two or more proper coordination equilibria, ones each agent strictly prefers, given the others' choices (Lewis 1969, ch. I).
Driving side, returning a call, meeting are all coordination games. Choosing a number is an anti-coordination game.
Empirical

Pareto optimality

Pareto dominance. Outcome \(o'\) dominates \(o\) iff it leaves at least one player better off and nobody worse off. Pareto optimal: an outcome not dominated by any other outcome.
The circled cells are the equilibria, and neither is Pareto optimal: \((3,3)\) is dominated by \((6,4)\), and \((2,2)\) by \((3,3)\). The tinted cells are the Pareto optima, and none is an equilibrium: at \((6,4)\), Player B walks away to take \(5\).
Stability and efficiency have come fully apart. Note that \((0,5)\) is Pareto optimal: optimality forbids free improvements and does not guarantee fairness.

Historical

Semantic meaning as coordination

Plato, c. 428 – 348 BC. Hermogenes, in the Cratylus: “No name belongs to a particular thing by nature” (384d). Cratylus holds the opposite.
Aristotle, 384 – 322 BC. “no name is a name naturally but only when it has become a symbol” (De Interpretatione, 16a).
Hume, 1711 – 1776. “Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, tho' they have never given promises to each other” (Treatise, 3.2.2). Languages, he adds in the same passage, arise the same way.
Saussure, 1857 – 1913. “The bond between the signifier and the signified is arbitrary” (Cours de linguistique générale, 1916).

Historical

von Neumann

John von Neumann
1903 – 1957
Budapest, Berlin, Princeton, Los Alamos.
Chess is mere computation. Poker turns on what your opponent thinks you will do: it involves guessing about other players' strategies.
1928 and 1944. The minimax theorem, then Theory of Games and Economic Behavior with Morgenstern.
The book constructs numerical utility from preferences over gambles.
The 1944 theory deals predominantly with competitive games, zero sum or otherwise, and has less to say on games where coordination is the central element.

Oberlin, Harvard, UCLA, Princeton.

Convention as equilibrium

Lewis's definition, and the knowledge it runs on.
Formal

Convention

A regularity \(R\) in the behaviour of a population \(P\) is a convention iff, and it is common knowledge that: everyone conforms to \(R\); everyone expects everyone else to conform; and everyone prefers to conform on condition that the others do, where \(R\) is one of at least two such regularities. Lewis 1969, ch. 1, condensed
Arbitrary. An alternative regularity would have served: left for right, wait for call.
Self-sustaining. The preference clause makes conforming a best response to conformity. Deviating alone only hurts the deviator.
No agreement. There is no requirement for people to sit down and explicitly coordinate. The behaviour emerges naturally from the dynamics of the game they are in.
Formal

Common knowledge

Mutual knowledge. Everyone knows \(p\). Silent on what anyone knows about anyone else.
Common knowledge. Everyone knows \(p\), everyone knows that everyone knows \(p\), and so on, all the way up.
Recursive mind-reading again: the poker levels, run all the way up the ladder.
