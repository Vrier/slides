Assessment Guidance

Writing an Essay

0

Aims of the essay

Your essay should defend a clear, substantive thesis about a phenomenon in pragmatics. It is not enough to summarise what the literature says: you should take a position and argue for it.

Typical moves that a strong essay might include:

  1. clarifying a theoretical claim and making it more precise;
  2. arguing against a particular proposal, by showing that some of its arguments fail or that it cannot handle certain cases;
  3. defending a proposal against one or more existing objections;
  4. providing positive reasons or new considerations in favour of a view;
  5. comparing two approaches and explaining which one we should prefer and why;
  6. developing or revising an existing account in light of a problem, whether conceptual, empirical, or methodological.

You do not have to do all of these, but you should do some of them in a sustained and structured way, with the essay organised around your central thesis rather than around individual readings.

1

Using the literature

Your essay should engage closely with the literature, which is likely (though not certain) to include the module's readings for your topic and further related work that you identify yourself. You should be selective and focus on the most relevant pieces for your argument.

Represent other authors accurately and charitably, and aim any objection at the strongest version of the view you are discussing.

2

Examples and data

You are strongly encouraged to:

  1. use concrete linguistic data (constructed examples, attested examples, corpus data, experimental results) to motivate and test theoretical claims;
  2. make it clear which examples are yours and which are taken from the literature;
  3. explain how your examples support or challenge the positions you discuss.

Number your examples and refer to them by number. When you rely on an experimental result, say briefly what the task was and what was measured.

3

Explaining technical notions

You will need to explain some technical notions in your essay (implicature, equilibrium, surprisal, prior, the rationality parameter). A reliable default recipe for a technical explanation:

  1. Compact statement. A short, precise statement using the appropriate technical terms.
  2. Plain gloss. The same idea in simpler language, as if you were explaining it to an intelligent non-specialist.
  3. Illustrative example. A clear case, often a minimal pair, that shows the mechanism in action.
  4. Link back. Why the notion matters for your thesis, and how you will use it.

This is not the only correct way to explain things, but it is a good default if you are unsure how to proceed.

4

Imagined readers

When writing, imagine yourself addressing three audiences at once:

  1. an intelligent non-linguist (your flatmate), who should be able to follow the big picture and the main examples;
  2. your classmates on this module, who know the tools and debates without having read everything you cite, and who should find the essay informative and easy to navigate;
  3. a specialist in pragmatics, who will notice whether you have represented the literature fairly, engaged with it critically, and drawn out the interesting consequences.

Your essay should be readable and valuable at all three levels.

5

What I look for

There is no separate marking rubric for this assessment. In broad terms, I look at the following.

Understanding. A solid grasp of the relevant theories, arguments, and empirical phenomena, with other authors' views reported accurately and charitably.

Argument. A clear thesis, arguments that actually bear on it, and plausible objections anticipated and addressed.

Evidence. Well-chosen examples and results, presented clearly, and claims supported by the evidence.

Structure. An organisation that is easy to follow, with an introduction and conclusion that make clear what the essay does and why, and transitions that keep the reader oriented.

Clarity. Prose that is clear, concise, and technically accurate, with notation and diagrams introduced and explained when needed.

Engagement. Your own perspective, synthesis, or small innovations, and some reflection on what the argument leaves open.

6

Writing advice

  1. Lead with your thesis. Early in the introduction, make it very clear what you are going to argue, and how the essay will proceed.
  2. Tell a story. Think of the essay as making one sustained case, rather than as a list of facts or a summary of readings. Each section should advance the case, and you should be able to say in one sentence what each section is for.
  3. Signpost clearly. Use section headings and explicit transitions, so that the reader always knows where they are in the argument and why each section is there.
  4. Be selective. Resist the urge to include everything you have read. A focused, well-developed argument about a narrower topic is almost always better than a broad but shallow survey. Examples, formulas, tables, and figures all count towards the 2,500 words, so choose them carefully.
  5. Revise for clarity. Leave time to re-read and edit, checking that your main line of thought is clearly visible and that each section contributes to it.
7

Academic integrity

You must follow the university's policies on academic integrity. All sources (articles, books, lecture notes, online resources) must be properly cited, and unacknowledged use of other people's words or ideas, including material produced by AI tools, counts as plagiarism.

You are welcome to discuss ideas with classmates, but the final structure, wording, and arguments of the essay must be your own. Any example taken from the literature must be clearly marked as such.

If you are unsure whether something counts as appropriate use of a resource, please ask.
