2

Questions on the presentation topics

Suggestions only. You can take a topic you did not present. The readings are on the presentation topics sheet.

  1. Hyperbole, irony and metaphor. The kettle cost a million pounds conveys that the kettle was expensive and that the speaker is annoyed. Kao, Wu, Bergen & Goodman (2014) derive both from a listener who infers the question under discussion. To model irony, Kao & Goodman (2015) had to add a second dimension of affect. Is inference about the question under discussion a general account of non-literal language, or does each figure of speech need ingredients of its own? Discuss one of hyperbole, irony or metaphor.
  2. Politeness and social meaning. A friend's poem is bad, and you tell them It wasn't terrible. Yoon, Tessler, Goodman & Frank (2020) explain the choice with three goals in the speaker's utility: to inform, to be kind, and to present oneself well. How would Brown & Levinson (1987) describe the same utterance? What does the formal account explain that theirs does not, and what does it leave out?
  3. Embedded implicatures. Every student read some of the books seems to have a reading on which every student read some but not all of them. Geurts & Pouscoulous (2009) found little evidence for such readings; Potts et al. (2016) found more. How strong is the evidence? Does it show that scalar implicature is computed in the grammar (Chierchia, Fox & Spector 2012), or can pragmatic reasoning derive the same readings?
  4. Pragmatic development in children. Young children accept Some of the horses jumped over the fence when all of them did. They do better when the alternatives are given in the context (Barner, Brooks & Bale 2011), when they can give graded judgments (Katsos & Bishop 2011), and with ad hoc implicatures (Stiller, Goodman & Frank 2015). What develops: the meanings of the words, access to the alternatives, the reasoning, or the ability to do the task?
  5. Relevance Theory against RSA. Coffee would keep me awake can accept an offer of coffee or decline it, depending on the context. How does a hearer arrive at each reading according to Relevance Theory? What would an RSA account of the same example have to specify? Are the two accounts rivals, or do they describe the same inference at different levels of explanation in Marr's sense?
  6. Language models and theory of mind. Kosinski (2024) reports that large language models pass false-belief tasks. Ullman (2023) shows that they fail when the task is changed slightly, for example by making the container transparent. What would count as evidence that a model reasons about other minds? What does your answer imply for theories on which pragmatic inference is reasoning about the speaker?
3

General questions

From logic to probability · week 1
  1. Some of the students passed suggests that some did not. Yet the speaker can go on: In fact, all of them did. The conclusion of a valid argument cannot be taken back like this. What kind of inference is a scalar implicature: a deduction with hidden premises, a default, or a probabilistic inference?
Game theory and signalling · week 2
  1. Russell and Quine objected that a language cannot have been set up by agreement, because agreeing already requires a language. Lewis (1969) replies that a convention is an equilibrium in a coordination game, held in place by common knowledge. Does the reply succeed for the meanings of words? Compare a word with a convention such as driving on the left.
  2. Black Bart killed the sheriff and Black Bart caused the sheriff to die can describe the same event, yet the second suggests an unusual killing. Can reasoning between a speaker and a hearer in a signalling game derive this (Horn 1984; Franke 2013)? What does the derivation have to assume about the costs of the two messages and about what counts as a typical killing?
Information theory · weeks 3–4
  1. Speakers choose maths over mathematics more often when the word is predictable from its context (Mahowald et al. 2013). They leave out that more often when the clause it introduces is predictable (Jaeger 2010). Are these choices made for the hearer? Compare the information-theoretic explanation with Grice's maxims of Quantity and Manner, or with Horn's (1984) Q and R principles.
Probability and priors · week 5
  1. Most people judge it more probable that Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement than that she is a bank teller (Tversky & Kahneman 1983). Is this an error in reasoning about probability, or a reasonable interpretation of the question (Dulany & Hilton 1991)? What follows for theories that treat hearers as Bayesian reasoners?
  2. A 1951 intelligence estimate called a Soviet attack on Yugoslavia a serious possibility. Its readers took the phrase to mean anything from a 20% to an 80% chance (Kent 1964). What do words such as possible, likely and almost certainly mean? How much of what they convey is literal meaning, and how much is implicature?
The Rational Speech Act framework · week 6
  1. A speaker has looked at only two of three apples and says Some of the apples are red. Listeners are then less ready to conclude that not all of them are (Goodman & Stuhlmüller 2013). How does Grice's reasoning explain this, and how does RSA? Is RSA a formalisation of Grice here, or a different theory?
  2. Nearly everyone infers not free from cheap. Hardly anyone infers not happy from content (van Tiel et al. 2016). Why do scalar inferences differ so much from one scale to another? Say what RSA and the grammatical account each have to assume about where the alternatives come from.
Cognition, relevance and grammar · week 8
  1. People take longer to judge Some elephants are mammals false than to judge it true (Bott & Noveck 2004). What does the extra time show about how scalar implicatures are computed? Compare what default accounts (Levinson 2000), Relevance Theory and the grammatical account predict, and consider how far the result depends on the task.
  2. Julian is Cuban. Did Sandra discover that Julian dances salsa? Participants rate the speaker as more certain that Julian dances salsa here than when the first sentence is Julian is German (Degen & Tonhauser 2021). Does this show that presupposition projection comes in degrees? Or is factivity all or nothing, with the gradience coming from prior beliefs and the task (Grove & White 2026)?
