How do we do things with words?

Some (most?) utterances do something other than describe the world.
A purely descriptive Semantic picture
An utterance reports a state of affairs, and we judge it true or false. We've already seen several cases last week where context and role in interaction come in.
This week

Performatives and felicity

Utterances judged as successful or failed acts in context rather than as true or false descriptions.
Decide what this utterance does
I now pronounce you husband and wife.an officiant, at a wedding
Empirical

Performatives

I now pronounce you husband and wife.an officiant, at a wedding
Not a report
You cannot reply That's false. Nothing is being described: before the words there is no marriage, and after them there is one.
Performative
Austin's name for utterances that perform the very act they name. In saying I pronounce, the officiant pronounces.
More performatives · first person, present tense
(1)
I apologise.
(2)
I promise to pay you tomorrow.
(3)
I hereby declare the meeting open.the hereby test: a performative still sounds natural with hereby inserted
Decide what this utterance does
I bet you sixpence it will rain tomorrow.J. L. Austin's own example
Empirical

Uptake

I bet you sixpence it will rain tomorrow.
Uptake
The bet exists only once you answer You're on. Some acts need the hearer's uptake to come off at all.
Evaluation
If it rains I win, and if not you do. But the bet itself was never true or false; it was on, or it was off.
In effect, or not in effect. Never true, never false.
Decide what this utterance does
I hereby declare you married.someone at a house party, glass raised
Empirical

A misfire

I hereby declare you married.someone at a house party, glass raised
What went wrong
Not falsity. There is no recognised procedure for house-party weddings, the speaker has no standing, and everyone treats it as a joke: the act simply fails to come off.
The diagnosis
The utterance is infelicitous. To say exactly when performatives fail, Austin wrote out conditions of felicity.
Empirical

Austin's felicity conditions

Six conditions, lettered A, B and Γ.
A · The procedure
A.1
There must exist an accepted conventional procedure with a conventional effect, including the uttering of certain words by certain persons in certain circumstances.
A.2
The particular persons and circumstances must be appropriate for the procedure invoked.
B · The execution
B.1
The procedure must be executed correctly…
B.2
…and completely.
Γ · The spirit
Γ.1
Participants must have the thoughts, feelings and intentions the procedure requires…
Γ.2
…and must subsequently conduct themselves accordingly.
Empirical

Misfires and abuses

Which condition breaks determines how the act fails.
A or B broken
Misfire
The act is null and void: it does not come off at all.
I hereby declare you married.the house-party wedding: no procedure, no authority. No marriage exists.
Γ broken
Abuse
The act comes off, but hollow: performed without the required spirit.
I promise to pay you tomorrow.said with no intention of paying. Insincere, but the promise was made, and you may hold me to it.
The intuition
Performatives have a dimension of assessment of their own: felicity rather than truth. That dimension has structure.

Historical
STRAND 02 / 04 · LI7862 · Week 2

Austin & Searle

Post-war Oxford ordinary-language philosophy, and the move from Austin's disruptive observations to Searle's systematic theory.
Historical

Ordinary-language Oxford

After the war, Oxford philosophers treated everyday usage as serious evidence about meaning.
The milieu
Austin, Grice and Strawson, with Searle arriving as a Rhodes Scholar: careful attention to what we actually say, when we would say it, and what saying it does.
Against the positivists
Where logical positivism dismissed unverifiable sentences as meaningless (week 1), the ordinary-language school asked what those sentences were for.
Saturday mornings
Austin ran weekly term-time discussions where philosophers worked through usage together: collective, meticulous, and suspicious of grand theory.
Historical

Austin: saying as doing

J. L. Austin
1911 – 1960 · Oxford
A classicist at Balliol, prize fellow of All Souls at 22, then tutor at Magdalen. He published almost nothing in his lifetime and died at 48; both books were reconstructed from his lectures.
The war
Intelligence Corps from 1940; from 1942 he directed the section (the Martians) that prepared the intelligence for the invasion of Western Europe. Warnock: “more than anybody responsible for the life-saving accuracy of the D-Day intelligence”. Left in 1945 a Lt-Colonel: OBE, Croix de Guerre, US Legion of Merit.
How to Do Things with Words
The 1955 William James Lectures at Harvard, published in 1962. Many utterances are actions performed in speaking rather than descriptions judged true or false.
Historical

John Searle

John Searle
1932 – 2025 · Berkeley
John Searle came to Oxford from the United States as a Rhodes Scholar and studied at Christ Church while Austin was lecturing. He spent his career at Berkeley, where his later work ranged over intentionality, consciousness (the Chinese Room), and institutional facts.
Speech Acts (1969)
Turned Austin's observations into a theory: every illocution analysed as a force applied to a content, governed by statable conditions.
The taxonomy (1976)
Classified every possible illocutionary act into five types, using three diagnostic dimensions.

Force, content & fit

Separating what an utterance is about from what it does: three acts, the force/content split, and the five illocutionary types.
Formal

One utterance, three acts

Austin splits any utterance into three simultaneous acts.
There's a bull in that field.
Locutionthe act of saying
Producing the words, with their sense and reference.
She uttered an English sentence about a bull and a field.
“She said that there is a bull in that field.”
Illocutionthe act in saying
What the utterance counts as: its force.
Speech-act theory lives here. This is the act we classify.
“She warned me not to cross.”
Perlocutionthe act by saying
The effects the utterance has on the hearer.
Not conventional: the same warning may reassure or terrify.
“She talked me into taking the long way round.”
Formal

Force over content

Every illocution splits into what it is about and what it does. Searle writes F(p): a force applied to a content.

Formal

How to promise

To promise that p is to satisfy four kinds of condition.
1
Propositional content: p predicates a future act A of the speaker S.
2
Preparatory: H would prefer S's doing A to S's not doing it; it is not obvious that S would do A anyway.
3
Sincerity: S intends to do A.
4
Essential: the utterance counts as the undertaking of an obligation to do A.
Constitutive rules
What makes an act a promise at all, rather than advice on promising well: constitutive rules, like the rules of chess, rather than regulative ones, like etiquette. Speech Acts (1969), Ch. 3.
Formal

Direction of fit

When words and world disagree, one of them is at fault.
↓
Word to world
The words must match the world. If they do not, the words are wrong: retract the claim.
assertions · predictions
↑
World to word
The world must be made to match the words. If it is not, the world is at fault: the order was disobeyed, the promise broken.
requests · promises
0
No fit
The fit is presupposed. The point is to express an attitude toward a content taken for granted.
thanks · apologies
Second dimension
Alongside the illocutionary point and the sincerity condition (the state expressed: belief, want, intention), direction of fit is one of Searle's three diagnostics for the taxonomy.

Formal

Two acts at once

Can you pass me the salt?a guest, at dinner
The literal act
A yes/no question about ability. It is really asked: the hearer can answer Yes and sit still. The literal answer shows the question was there.
The primary act
A request, performed by way of the question. Searle (1975): the secondary act is performed literally, the primary act indirectly, and the hearer must compute one from the other.
Spot the primary act

(6)

Formal

Indirection is systematic

Indirect requests are not random. Each one targets a felicity condition of the direct request it stands in for.

The pattern
Each route mentions one condition on the direct act; the hearer reasons from the condition to the act. Searle (1975); Gordon & Lakoff (1975).
Formal

Convention or inference

Can you…? as a request: by rule, or by reasoning.
Sadock (1974)
Idiom
A speech-act idiom, like kick the bucket: the request meaning is listed in the lexicon rather than computed.
Can you please close the window?please fits the conventional form only: ?Are you able to please close the window?
Searle (1975)
Inference
Computed from the literal act, shared background and cooperation. The same routes recur across languages; idioms would not predict that.
Can you pass me the salt? Yes.the literal act stays available; an idiom would have swallowed it.

Jennifer Saul

Jennifer Saul
b. 1968 · Waterloo
American philosopher of language: BA Rochester, PhD Princeton under Scott Soames. Over twenty years at Sheffield; since 2019 Waterloo Chair in Social and Political Philosophy of Language. Director of the Society for Women in Philosophy UK (2009–2019); founder of the Feminist Philosophers blog.
Lying, Misleading, and What Is Said (2012)

Implicit bias
Director of the Leverhulme Implicit Bias and Philosophy project (2011–2013).

The pragmatics of racist and deceptive political speech: overt, covert and protean dogwhistles; figleaves.
Applied

Dogwhistles as communicative acts

An utterance engineered to perform different acts for different audiences.
Overt
The target meaning is accessible to all hearers, but its significance goes unrecognised by outsiders.
Covert
The target meaning itself is concealed from at least part of the audience.
The common core
Audience segmentation plus deniability: both kinds rely on concealment from some hearers, and the speaker can always retreat to the innocent reading.
Read as speech acts
One locution, two intended illocutions: an innocuous act for the broad audience, a signal of allegiance for the target audience. Saul (2018; 2024).
Applied

Defusing, misfire, backfire

Publicly naming a dogwhistle can defuse it, but only under conditions: felicity conditions, again.
Norm acceptance
Defusing works only where the audience shares a norm against explicit prejudice, so that exposure carries a cost.
Target match
…and only where the audience agrees on who the target is. Protean dogwhistles shift targets, so both conditions wobble and defusing fails. Saul (2019).
Misfire
The target audience misses the signal: nothing beyond the innocent reading is communicated. The covert act does not come off.
Backfire
The broad audience recognises the signal: deniability collapses, and the speaker owns the act in public.
Applied

Figleaves

The dogwhistle's partner device: it covers the message rather than hiding it.
Figleaves at work
(11)
I'm not racist, but…
(12)
Just asking questions.
(13)
Many people are saying…
What it does
Attaches to a message that would otherwise be rejected as norm-violating, and supplies just enough cover for conflicted hearers to accept it. The message stays in full view.
Against the dogwhistle
A dogwhistle conceals the act; a figleaf performs it openly and buys deniability another way. Same goal, opposite mechanism. Each covered utterance shifts what the audience treats as sayable. Saul (2024), Dogwhistles and Figleaves.

Applied

Machine speech acts

I sincerely apologise for the confusion.a chatbot, having invented a citation
Run the checklist
The form is perfect: first person, present tense, passes the hereby test. But the Γ-conditions want a state to express; a promise wants an obligation undertaken; an assertion wants a speaker who can be held to it.
No assertion
Butlin & Viebahn (2024): asserting requires descriptive function and liability to sanction. Current systems fail the second.
Proto-assertion
Williams & Bayne (2024): a category between mere output and full assertion, as with children entering the practice.
Chatbot apologies
Magnus, Buccella & D'Cruz (2025): chatbot apologies keep the form while nothing satisfies the Γ-conditions.
