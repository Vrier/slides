Explicature

The rows already filled in are models to follow, and short notes are enough in the cells. Only the explicatures and the questions need full sentences.

Definitions
ExplicatureA communicated proposition that the hearer recovers by developing the decoded meaning of the sentence through inference.
SaturationFilling a slot that the sentence provides, such as the referent of he.
Free enrichmentAdding content that no word in the sentence calls for, such as from that cut in You’re not going to die, said to a child crying over a cut finger.
Ad hoc conceptA concept built during comprehension from the concept a word encodes, with a narrower or a broader extension. It is written in capitals with an asterisk, as TIRED*.
NarrowingThe ad hoc concept covers less than the encoded one, as TIRED* in I’m tired. Let’s go to the cinema, too tired to go on working.
BroadeningThe ad hoc concept covers more than the encoded one, as RAW* in This steak is raw, cooked too little to eat.
Higher-level explicatureA description of the speech act or attitude, with the explicature embedded in it, as in Mary is telling Peter that …

For each expression, say what the words leave open, write the explicature of the sentence it is in as a full sentence with the gaps filled in, and name the processes that get from the words to the explicature.

Four checks find the processes. Is there a word such as it, that or No whose content comes from the context? Filling it in is saturation. Is a word used more narrowly or more loosely than usual? That gives an ad hoc concept, written in capitals with an asterisk and a few words on what it covers. Does the hearer add something that no word calls for, such as when, where or which one? That is free enrichment. Is the speaker doing something by speaking, such as apologising? The higher-level explicature says so.

Effects and effort

Definitions
Positive cognitive effectA worthwhile difference to a person’s representation of the world, such as a true conclusion.
Processing effortThe effort of perception, memory and inference that it takes to derive the effects. A longer, rarer or more complex input takes more.
RelevanceOther things being equal, the greater the positive cognitive effects of an input, the more relevant it is, and the greater the processing effort, the less relevant it is.

Each row sets what was said beside something the speaker could have said instead. In some rows the alternative says the same thing in other words, and in others it is a different answer. Optimal relevance

Definitions
Optimal relevanceAn utterance is optimally relevant to a hearer if and only if (a) it is relevant enough to be worth the hearer’s effort to process, and (b) it is the most relevant one that the speaker’s abilities and preferences allow.
Clause (b)The hearer of I have already written a third of the paper takes the speaker to have written only a third. Two thirds would have been more relevant, and nothing prevented her from saying so.
Expectation of relevanceThe hearer’s expectation about how an utterance will be relevant. After a question, the questioner expects an answer to it.
AbilitiesWhat the speaker is able to say, given what she knows and can truthfully claim.
PreferencesWhat the speaker is willing to say.

Explicature or implicature

Definitions
ImplicatureA communicated proposition that is not an explicature. Every implicature is an implicated premise or an implicated conclusion.
Implicated premiseA contextual assumption that the speaker intends the hearer to supply. After Did John pay back the money he owed you?, the answer No. He forgot to go to the bank needs the premise that forgetting to go to the bank can leave a person unable to repay money.
Implicated conclusionA conclusion that the speaker intends the hearer to draw from the explicature and the context together. Here, that John could not repay because he forgot to go to the bank.
Strong implicatureAn implicature that the hearer has to recover for the utterance to satisfy the expectation of relevance. That conclusion is one, since without it the answer explains nothing.
Weak implicatureOne of a range of similar conclusions that the utterance encourages, none of which the hearer has to draw. The hearer takes part of the responsibility for it. That John may repay when he next goes to the bank is one.
Extra effortA form that costs the hearer more effort than a simpler one is taken to offer extra effects in return. Black Bart caused the sheriff to die conveys that the killing was indirect.

Questions

Discuss these with a partner and write a few sentences on each, with rows from your tables as evidence.

  1. Several inferences that Horn and Levinson treat as implicatures belong to the explicature for Relevance Theory. Does it matter which side of the line an inference falls on? Think about what a speaker can later say they never said, as Johnson does in Q4.
  2. Taking the data as a whole, how well does Relevance Theory explain what questioners do when an answer gives less than they asked for? When do they ask again, and when do they draw a conclusion?
  3. For Relevance Theory only true conclusions are positive cognitive effects. Is that a useful way of thinking about a statement that later proves false?
  4. Think of a time someone answered your question with something other than what you asked. What did you conclude, and what in the answer led you there?
