The Research Project

The task

Weighting100%.
LengthLimit 3,500 words. Excludes references, the data appendix, and data quoted in the body (numbered examples and transcript extracts).
DeadlineMonday 14 December 2026.
SubmissionVia Blackboard, as a PDF or Word document.
ReferencingAny standard academic style (APA, Chicago, Linguistic Inquiry), used consistently.

Produce a small-scale pragmatics project based on a dataset that you assemble yourself. Written up as a research paper. The project has three requirements: a focused question about a pragmatic phenomenon in a real setting; a small dataset of real use that bears on the question; and an analysis applied from a theoretical framework from the course, with the choice of framework explicitly justified.

The project combines theoretical framing with empirical analysis. A project that only describes a dataset is not enough for high marks, it must be made clear why the particular framework has been applied to the data.

Writing guidance and the marking criteria are in the companion document, Writing the Research Project.

2

Your dataset

The project must be based on a dataset that you assemble yourself. Some examples of suitable datasets:

  1. a short recorded interaction, or a short naturally occurring conversation;
  2. a short institutional exchange (a service encounter, a consultation, a meeting);
  3. a small set of headlines, advertisements, slogans, speeches, interview extracts, or public notices;
  4. a short set of written or digital interactions (messages, emails, forum threads);
  5. a small set of language-model outputs, if your question concerns machine communication;
  6. a short fictional extract, if your question concerns how pragmatic phenomena are represented in stylised discourse.

Size. Keep it small. As a rough guide: one to four minutes of talk, or a short extract from a longer interaction; six to twenty short written examples, depending on length and complexity. A smaller, clearer dataset that you analyse closely is worth more than a larger one that you cannot.

Provenance. Record where every example comes from: source and date for public data; model, version, date, and prompt for language-model outputs; for your own recordings, keep the audio.

3

Shape of the write-up

Introduction~400 words. The question, and the answer the project will argue for.
Framework~500 words.
Data~400 words. The dataset, its context, and how it was collected.
Analysis~1,800 words.
Conclusion~400 words. What the data show, and the limits of the analysis.

This is only indicative guidance for expectations. Your own sections and wordcounts may vary wildly from this.

4

Ethics

Data collection for this project falls under the university's and the School's research-ethics guidance; read it before you collect, and follow it. In particular: anonymise names and identifying details in the write-up and the appendix; include no sensitive personal information; if you record private interaction, obtain consent appropriately. Publicly available datasets like media transcripts or political speeches are unlikely to raise ethics concerns. At least in terms of collection!

5

What to submit

  1. a cover sheet;
  2. the project itself, within the 3,500-word limit;
  3. a short appendix containing your data, a brief description of its context, and its provenance.
6

Project options

Some examples of projects that may be appropriate. You are very much encouraged to come up with your own. They will likely be better if you have personal knowledge or connection to the subject!

Speech acts · week 2
  1. Institutional declarations. Choose one institution that runs on declarations (a referee in GAA or football, a courtroom, the chair of a debate or of the Dáil) and analyse three of its central acts: the authority each requires, what counts as uptake, how each can misfire, and who can challenge it.
  2. Direct and indirect requests. Collect requests from one setting (a shared house, a café counter, a tutorial) and analyse when speakers choose direct forms and when indirect ones, and whether the indirection is read by convention or by inference.
  3. Dogwhistles and figleaves. Take a small set of public political utterances (Irish, British, and European politics all supply them) and analyse them as communicative acts: surface meaning, target meaning, the uptake each audience supplies, and what deniability amounts to when it fails.
  4. Machine speech acts. Take a small set of chatbot outputs and assess them against the felicity conditions for asserting or promising: what commitment and uptake amount to when the speaker is a machine.
Implicature · week 3
  1. Humour and flouting. Take a short clip from a sitcom or panel show (Derry Girls, Father Ted, and the panel news quizzes are rich sources) and analyse which maxims carry each joke, and whether the departures are open flouts, concealed violations, or something more complex.
  2. Said and meant. Take one real exchange and separate the proposition expressed from what is inferred, reconstructing the hearer's route step by step.
  3. Generalised and particularised. Build a small dataset containing both default-like implicatures (some, or, warm) and heavily context-dependent ones, and analyse what context contributes to each.
Presupposition · week 4
  1. Presupposition in public discourse. Take a short political speech, party broadcast, or news report and analyse how background assumptions are packaged as already settled.
  2. Trigger types in headlines and notices. Build a small dataset of definites, factives, aspectuals, iteratives, and clefts from headlines or public notices and analyse what each foregrounds and backgrounds.
  3. Assert or presuppose. Collect examples where contested content could have been asserted directly and was presupposed instead, and analyse what the packaging buys the speaker.
Common ground & projection · week 5
  1. Challenge and accommodation. Take an adversarial exchange (broadcast political interviews and Leaders' Questions are ideal) and analyse where presuppositions are challenged, where they slip into the common ground, and what makes challenge easy or hard.
  2. Informative presupposition. Collect cases where new content rides in presuppositionally (announcements, signage, the lift is out of order again) and analyse when accommodation succeeds and when it invites resistance.
  3. Projection in the wild. Collect real sentences that embed triggers under negation, questions, conditionals, and attitude verbs, and test whether projection follows the textbook pattern.
Information · week 6
  1. Efficiency in a register. Take one compressed register (headlines, texting, live sports commentary, radiotelephony) and analyse its omissions, abbreviations, and orderings as information-theoretic design.
  2. Predictability and omission. Take a compressed register and analyse what it leaves out against what the reader recovers: which material is predictable enough to drop, and what happens at the points where an omission bites.
  3. Redundancy under noise. Take safety-critical or noisy-channel talk (aviation readbacks, spelling alphabets on a call, station announcements) and analyse its repetitions and confirmations as error correction.
Politeness · week 8
  1. Raw and redressed. Collect requests, complaints, refusals, or corrections from one setting and analyse how the same act is performed bald on record and with redress, and what drives the choice.
  2. One institution. Choose office hours, a workplace, a classroom, or a service encounter and analyse how power, distance, and imposition shape utterance design there. Service encounters, with their heavy mitigation (sorry, could I just, you're grand), are a rich site.
  3. Slagging and mock impoliteness. Take banter among friends or teammates and analyse whether the surface impoliteness threatens face or builds solidarity, and whether the classical model stretches to it or a successor account is needed.
Neo-Gricean pragmatics · week 9
  1. Scalar inference in the wild. Collect scalar inferences (some, or, warm, three) from real discourse and analyse them with scales and the Q and R principles: which enrichments arise, which stay suspended, and what in the context decides it.
  2. The division of pragmatic labour. Collect pairs where a marked form sits beside a plainer rival (not unhappy beside happy, cause the car to stop beside stop the car) and analyse how the marked choice acquires a marked meaning.
Relevance Theory · week 10
  1. Relevance and the headline. Take a set of headlines or clickbait and analyse them through the trade-off of processing effort and cognitive effect: what the reader is made to spend, and what the headline promises back.
  2. Enrichment in short messages. Collect texts, emails, or chat turns and analyse how explicatures and implicatures are recovered: what is decoded, what is inferred, and how far the enrichment runs.
Rational Speech Acts · week 11
  1. Choosing the referring expression. Collect referring expressions from a cooperative task (giving directions, ordering at a counter, describing an item) and build a small RSA model of the speaker's choice among alternatives, checking its predictions against what speakers actually said.
  2. Hyperbole. Collect everyday exaggerations (reviews, complaints, match commentary) and analyse them through RSA's treatment of non-literal language: what a literally false utterance conveys about degree and affect, and why the listener never takes it literally.
Grammaticalism · week 12
  1. Implicature in embedded positions. Hunt for real sentences where an enrichment sits under negation, every, or if, and analyse whether a grammatical exhaustification account or a global pragmatic account fits them. Fair warning: such cases are rare in natural data, and part of the project is saying what your finds can and cannot decide.
  2. Free choice in rules and permissions. Collect permissions with or and any from signs, rules, and terms of use (you may take an apple or a pear) and analyse the free-choice reading through exhaustification.

A project built on one of the four programmes should include at least some comparison and contrast with at least one other competing framework: say what a rival account makes of your best cases, and where the two come apart.
