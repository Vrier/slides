Assessment Guidance

Writing the Research Project

0

Aims of the project

Your project should defend a clear, substantive thesis about your data. It is not enough to summarise what the literature says: you should take a position and argue for it.

Some typical moves that a strong project might make:

  1. clarifying a theoretical claim, making it more precise, and testing the precise version on your data;
  2. arguing that your chosen framework handles your cases well, or showing exactly where it struggles;
  3. defending an account against an existing objection, using your examples as evidence;
  4. comparing two approaches on your examples and explaining which one we should prefer and why;
  5. developing or revising an account in light of a problem that your data raise.

You do not have to do all of these, but you should do some of them in a sustained and structured way, with the project organised around your central thesis rather than around individual readings.

1

Framework

Your write-up should contain a section, placed before the analysis, that names your framework and justifies the choice; around 400 to 600 words is usually about right. State the framework's central claims in your own words, explain why it is a good fit for your question and your kind of data, and say what it leads you to expect for examples like yours. A short comparison with a rival framework is optional but very welcome, and if your project is built on one of the four programmes from weeks 9 to 12, some comparison with at least one competing framework is expected.

2

Collecting and presenting data

Plan small. Two or three minutes of talk will keep you occupied for many hours, and an hour of multi-party conversation can take days or even weeks to transcribe accurately. You should limit what you collect to what you can practically transcribe and usefully analyse, and expect to select a shorter target extract from a longer recording.

The exception to this is if you are using a large and easily accessible corpus for data analysis: there the collection is already done, and the limit is what you can meaningfully analyse.

The observer's paradox. Recording changes talk. If people know they are being recorded, they are unlikely to produce their natural speech style, and friends who are told to have a natural conversation while you record them will produce anything but. It is worth designing around this. Public discourse, institutional talk that is recorded anyway, and written data avoid the problem entirely.

Pilot. Whenever possible, do a small trial collection first, to check that the recording is audible, that the examples are transcribable, and that the data actually bear on your question. It is much better to discover a problem at this stage than after you have collected everything.

Transcription. Use ordinary orthography. Mark pauses, overlap, and intonation only to the level of detail that your analysis actually uses, and give a key for every convention you adopt. A politeness project may need very little of this; a project on interruption needs overlap marked precisely.

Presentation. Number your examples and refer to them in the analysis by number. Your reader should never have to hunt through the appendix to follow a claim: the example under discussion should appear in the text, with the appendix holding the full record.

Grundy 2008. Doing Pragmatics, 3rd edn, ch. 12.

3

Using the literature

Your project should engage closely with the literature, which is likely (though not certain) to include the module's readings for your framework and further related work that you identify yourself. Be selective: the project is an argument, and every citation should serve it. There is no credit for a long reference list of works you have not really used.

Represent other authors accurately and charitably, and aim any objection at the strongest version of the view you are discussing.

4

Explaining technical notions

You will need to explain some technical notions in your project (accommodation, projection, face, surprisal, alternatives). A reliable default recipe for a technical explanation:

  1. Compact statement. A short, precise statement using the appropriate technical terms.
  2. Plain gloss. The same idea in simpler language, as if you were explaining it to an intelligent non-specialist.
  3. Illustrative example. A clear case, often a minimal pair, that shows the mechanism in action.
  4. Link back. Why the notion matters for your thesis, and how you will use it.

This is not the only correct way to explain things, but it is a good default if you are unsure how to proceed.

5

Imagined readers

When writing, imagine yourself addressing three audiences at once:

  1. an intelligent non-linguist (your flatmate), who should be able to follow the big picture and the main examples;
  2. your classmates on this module, who know the tools and debates without having read everything you cite, and who should find the project informative and easy to navigate;
  3. a specialist in pragmatics, who will notice whether you have represented the literature fairly, engaged with it critically, and drawn out the interesting consequences.

Your project should be readable and valuable at all three levels.

6

What I look for

There is no separate marking rubric for this assessment. In broad terms, I look at the following.

Understanding. A solid grasp of the framework, the phenomenon, and the empirical territory, with other authors' views reported accurately and charitably.

The framework choice. A justification argued on the data, and a framework that then does real work: the analysis should look different because this particular framework is driving it.

Argument. A clear thesis, arguments that actually bear on it, and plausible objections anticipated and addressed.

Evidence. A well-chosen, manageable dataset; examples presented clearly and analysed closely; claims that the data actually support; a hard case chosen honestly and put to work.

Structure. An organisation that is easy to follow, with an introduction and conclusion that make clear what the project does and why, and transitions that keep the reader oriented.

Clarity. Prose that is clear, concise, and technically accurate, with notation and diagrams introduced and explained when needed.

Engagement. Something of your own: your perspective, synthesis, or small innovations, together with honest reflection on what the analysis leaves open.

7

Writing advice

  1. Lead with your question. Early in the introduction, make it very clear what you are going to argue or show, and how the project will proceed.
  2. Tell a story. Think of the project as making one sustained case, rather than as a list of facts or a summary of readings. Each section should advance the case, and you should be able to say in one sentence what each section is for.
  3. Signpost clearly. Use section headings and explicit transitions, so that the reader always knows where they are in the argument and why each section is there.
  4. Be selective. Resist the urge to include everything you have read. A focused, well-developed argument about a narrower topic is almost always better than a broad but shallow survey.
  5. Revise for clarity. Leave time to re-read and edit, checking that your main line of thought is clearly visible and that each section contributes to it.
8

Academic integrity

You must follow the university's policies on academic integrity. All sources (articles, books, lecture notes, online resources) must be properly cited, and unacknowledged use of other people's words or ideas, including material produced by AI tools, counts as plagiarism.

You are welcome to discuss ideas with classmates, but the final structure, wording, and arguments of the project must be your own. The dataset must be your own collection, and any example taken from the literature must be clearly marked as such.

If you are unsure whether something counts as appropriate use of a resource, please ask.
