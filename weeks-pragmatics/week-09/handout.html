Neo-Gricean Pragmatics

0

Grice’s maxims and notation

Definitions
What is saidThe content a sentence expresses by its conventional meaning.
ImplicatureWhat a speaker conveys beyond what is said. The hearer works it out by assuming the speaker is cooperative. An implicature can be cancelled without contradiction.
Cooperative PrincipleMake your contribution such as is required by the accepted purpose of the talk exchange. The maxims below all serve it.
Quantity 1Make your contribution as informative as is required.
Quantity 2Do not make your contribution more informative than is required.
QualityTry to make your contribution one that is true. Do not say what you believe to be false, or that for which you lack adequate evidence.
RelationBe relevant.
MannerBe perspicuous. 1 Avoid obscurity of expression. 2 Avoid ambiguity. 3 Be brief. 4 Be orderly.
Generalised implicatureAn implicature an expression carries in most contexts, unless something blocks it.
Particularised implicatureAn implicature that depends on the particular context of the utterance.
Notation
p +> qSaying p implicates q.
p entails qq is true whenever p is.
⟨all, some⟩A scale, written strongest first. A sentence with all entails the same sentence with some.
¬p, p ∧ qNot p. p and q.
KS pThe speaker knows that p.
PS pFor all the speaker knows, p. The same as ¬KS¬p.

Grice 1975. Logic and conversation. Syntax and Semantics 3: 41–58.

Implicatures

SentenceImplicated
(1)Some of the students passed.Not all of them passed.
(2)I believe she’s in.The speaker does not know that she is in.
(3)If you mow the lawn, I’ll give you five dollars.If you don’t, I won’t.
(4)She turned the key and the engine started.Then, and because she turned it, the engine started.
(5)I don’t think she’ll come.I think she won’t come.
(6)Black Bart caused the sheriff to die.He did not kill him in the ordinary, direct way.
(7a)I broke a finger yesterday.My own finger.
(7b)X is meeting a woman this evening.Someone other than his wife.
Definitions
Upper-bounding implicatureAn implicature that limits what is said from above. (1) says that at least some passed, and implicates that not all did. So do (2) and (7b).
Lower-bounding implicatureAn implicature that strengthens what is said towards a more specific, stereotypical case. (7a) says that some finger was broken, and implicates that it was the speaker’s own. So do (3), (4) and (5).
Conditional perfectionReading if as only if, as in (3).
Conjunction buttressingReading and as and then, or and as a result, as in (4).
Negative strengtheningReading don’t think as think not, as in (5).

(7a) and (7b) have the same form, with implicatures in opposite directions. Grice noted both, and called (7a) a reverse implicature. His maxims do not say when each arises. (6) is taken up in section 2.

Marked forms

Definitions
Unmarked expressionThe shorter, more usual way of saying something.
Marked expressionA longer or more roundabout way of saying much the same thing.
Stereotypical situationThe most typical situation an expression describes. Killing someone directly. Stopping a car with the brake pedal.
  1. (8a)Bill stopped the car.
  2. (8b)Bill caused the car to stop.
  3. (9a)The flag is pink.
  4. (9b)The flag is pale red.
  5. (10a)It’s likely that she’ll win.
  6. (10b)It’s not unlikely that she’ll win.
  7. (11a)My brother went to school.
  8. (11b)My brother went to the school.

Each (b) form is marked, and conveys that the situation is not the stereotypical one. (8b): Bill stopped the car in some unusual way, perhaps with the handbrake. (9b): a shade that pink does not cover. (10b): a more guarded estimate than (10a). (11b): a visit to the building, perhaps for a meeting. In (6), Black Bart might have tampered with the sheriff’s gun so that it backfired.

Horn’s Q and R principles

Definitions
Speaker’s economyThe pressure to say as little as possible. Taken to its limit, one word for every meaning.
Auditor’s economyThe hearer’s economy. The pressure to be told everything explicitly. Taken to its limit, a separate word for each meaning.
Q principleMake your contribution sufficient. Say as much as you can, given R. Hearer-based. It collects Quantity 1 and Manner 1 and 2.
R principleMake your contribution necessary. Say no more than you must, given Q. Speaker-based. It collects Quantity 2, Relation and Manner 3 and 4.
Q-based implicatureUpper-bounding. The speaker said no more, so nothing stronger holds.
R-based implicatureLower-bounding. The speaker said no more than was needed, so the hearer fills in the stereotypical case.
Division of pragmatic labourWhere a marked and an unmarked expression could say the same thing, the unmarked one is used for the stereotypical situation, by R. The marked one implicates, by Q, that the situation is not stereotypical.
Partial blockingThe marked form survives with the meaning the unmarked form leaves over. Pale red beside pink.

Zipf (1949) saw a vocabulary as a compromise between the two economies. Horn’s Q principle corresponds to the auditor’s economy and his R principle to the speaker’s. Quality stays outside both principles.

Example
  1. (1). All was available and more informative. By Q, the speaker would have said all if she could. So not all.
  2. (4). And then, as a result would say more than is needed. By R, the hearer fills in the usual order and connection.
  3. (6). Killed was available and shorter. By the division of pragmatic labour, caused to die implicates a killing that was not direct.
In the lexicon
Q-based narrowingA general term comes to exclude what a more specific term covers. Finger often excludes the thumb, and rectangle the square.
R-based narrowingA general term narrows to its stereotypical case. Drink for alcoholic drink.
R-based broadeningA specific term widens to its whole category. Hoover for any vacuum cleaner.
*nallLanguages have single words for all, no and some, and none that means not all. Some already implicates not all by Q, so such a word would add little.

Zipf 1949. Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort. Addison-Wesley.

Horn 1984. Toward a new taxonomy for pragmatic inference. In Schiffrin (ed.), Meaning, Form, and Use in Context: 11–42.

Levinson’s heuristics

Definitions
Q-heuristicWhat isn’t said, isn’t. From Quantity 1.
I-heuristicWhat is expressed simply is stereotypically exemplified. From Quantity 2.
M-heuristicWhat’s said in an abnormal way isn’t normal. From Manner: avoid obscurity, be brief.
PriorityWhere the heuristics conflict, Q-implicatures defeat M-implicatures, and M-implicatures defeat I-implicatures. Q > M > I.

Scalar and clausal implicature

Definitions
Horn scaleExpressions of the same kind ordered by strength, ⟨S, W⟩. A sentence with S entails the same sentence with W.
Conditions on scalesScale-mates are equally lexicalised, and from the same field and register. Some is compared with all. It is not compared with some but not all.
Scalar implicatureSaying a sentence with W implicates that the speaker does not know the sentence with S to be true: ¬KS S.
CompetenceThe assumption that the speaker knows whether the sentence with S is true. With competence, the scalar implicature becomes KS¬S.
Clausal implicatureWhere a stronger form would entail an embedded clause p and the weaker form used does not, the speaker implicates PS p ∧ PS¬p: she does not know whether p.
Clausal pairs⟨know p, believe p⟩. ⟨since p, q; if p, q⟩. ⟨p and q; p or q⟩.
NegationNegation reverses a scale. Not all of them came implicates that some did.
Example
  1. (1) Some of the students passed. Scale ⟨all, some⟩. Stronger sentence: All of the students passed.
  2. Scalar implicature: ¬KS(all passed). With competence: KS¬(all passed).
  3. (2) I believe she’s in. Pair ⟨know p, believe p⟩, with p = she’s in. I know she’s in would entail p.
  4. Clausal implicature: PS p ∧ PS¬p. She may be in, and she may not.

Increments and cancellation

Definitions
ContextThe propositions taken for granted at a point in the conversation. Written c.
ConsistentA proposition is consistent with c if it and everything in c can be true together.
IncrementationWhat an utterance conveys is added to c in a fixed order: entailments, then clausal implicatures, then scalar implicatures, then M-implicatures, then I-implicatures. Each is added only if it is consistent with c so far.
CancellationAn implicature is cancelled when it is not added, because it is inconsistent with c. No contradiction results.
  1. (19)Some of the students passed; in fact, all of them did.
Example
  1. Entailments. All of the students passed. Added.
  2. Clausal implicatures. None.
  3. Scalar implicature of some: KS¬(all passed). Inconsistent with step 1. Not added.
  4. M- and I-implicatures. None.

Defaults

Definitions
Default inferenceAn inference drawn unless something blocks it, without working out the particular speaker’s intentions.
Presumptive meaningLevinson’s term for a generalised implicature seen as a default.
Sentence meaningWhat a sentence means by its words and structure. The province of semantics.
Utterance-type meaningWhat an utterance of a given form normally conveys. Generalised implicatures belong here.
Utterance-token meaningWhat a particular utterance conveys in its context. Particularised implicatures belong here.
Default ruleA : B / C. From A, conclude C, provided B can consistently be assumed.
Non-monotonic inferenceInference in which adding a premise can withdraw a conclusion.
Pragmatic intrusionAn implicature that contributes to what is said.
Grice’s circleWhat is said seems to depend on implicature, and implicature depends on what is said.
Example
  1. Bird(t) : Flies(t) / Flies(t). Tweety is a bird, so Tweety flies.
  2. Add Penguin(t). Flies(t) can no longer be consistently assumed. The conclusion is withdrawn.
  3. some : not all / not all. From (1), not all of the students passed.
  4. Add in fact, all of them did. Not all is withdrawn, as in section 6.
  1. (22)If the old king has died of a heart attack and a republic has been declared, then Tom will be quite content.
  2. (23)If a republic has been declared and the old king has died of a heart attack, then Tom will be quite content.

And in (22) and (23) is read as and then. The reading is an I-implicature, and it falls inside the if-clause, so (22) and (23) can differ in truth value. For Levinson, generalised implicatures are defaults that arise locally, before the whole sentence has been heard, and so can feed what is said.

Heuristics and rational models

Definitions
BottleneckSpeech transmits about 7 syllables per second, under 100 bits of information. Inference is much faster, and hearers make up the difference with it.
HeuristicA fast shortcut that gives the right answer in the usual case. Levinson’s Q, I and M are heuristics.
Bounded rationalityAgents with limited time and information settle for options that are good enough.
CalculabilityGrice’s requirement that an implicature can be worked out by an argument, even when it is grasped intuitively.
Rational modelA model in which the hearer works out which utterance a rational speaker would choose in each state of affairs.
Default viewThe implicature is drawn at once. Cancelling it, where the context requires, costs time.
Context-driven viewThe literal meaning comes first. The implicature is drawn when the context supports it, and drawing it costs time.
Statenonesomeall

In each state a rational speaker chooses the most informative true utterance. Some is chosen only when not all passed, so a hearer who hears some infers not all.

  1. (24)Some elephants are mammals.

Bott & Noveck 2004. Participants judged sentences like (24) true or false. Those told to read some as some but not all answered over 600 ms more slowly than those told to read it as at least some. Under a 900 ms deadline there were fewer false answers than with 3 seconds. Both results count against the default view.

van Tiel et al. 2016. Across 43 scales, the rate of scalar implicature ran from 4% for ⟨happy, content⟩ to 100% for ⟨free, cheap⟩. The default view expects every scale to behave alike.

  7. Contrast what a default account and a rational account predict about an implicature and the cost of drawing it.
