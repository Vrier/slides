Shared background

Common ground, accommodation and projection.

Anoushka is a mountain guide. The local university has sent the six faculty members of its logic department on a beginners’ mountaineering course. This is deeply frustrating for Anoushka because, although they are all perfect logicians, they are novice climbers.

One important skill for any climber is tying the knot that attaches them to the rope. It is often hard to check your own knot, because it sits under your jacket, but easy to see other climbers’ knots, so climbers often check each other’s.

Anoushka teaches the six logicians to tie a figure-of-eight knot and asks them each to tie one. She then gathers them round to see whether they have done it correctly. Each logician can see every other logician’s knot, but not their own. Anoushka notices, to her frustration, that not all of the knots are right. She shakes her head and announces: “Well, at least one of you hasn’t tied your knot correctly. Without looking at your own knot, raise your hand if you know that your knot is tied incorrectly.” The logicians look around at each other and all shake their heads. “How about now?” says Anoushka. Again they look around despondently. “How about now?” she asks a third time, and suddenly some of the logicians raise their hands.

How many logicians had tied their knots correctly? Justify your answer.

Empirical

Common knowledge and common ground

Common knowledge. A proposition is common knowledge in a group when everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without limit. Lewis introduced the notion in his account of convention, and Aumann gave it its formal definition.
Common ground. For Stalnaker, the propositions the participants in a conversation mutually accept for its purposes. The acceptance is iterated in the same way, and it can fall short of belief, as when people assume something for the sake of argument.
The common ground changes as the conversation goes on. An assertion that is accepted adds its content to it, and what a speaker presupposes has to be in it already.
Lewis (1969), Convention · Aumann (1976), Annals of Statistics 4 · Stalnaker (2002), Linguistics and Philosophy 25.
Empirical

Presupposed and asserted

The presuppositions of an utterance are what its speaker takes for granted, or acts as if they take for granted, for the utterance to make sense in its context. Some come from the context and the norms of conversation. Others are carried by particular words and constructions, the presupposition triggers.

Empirical

Presupposition and the common ground

(7)My mother is coming to pick me up. I have a sister, by the way.
A presupposition is content that the speaker treats as already in the common ground. My mother presupposes that the speaker has a mother, which the common ground already contains, so the first sentence only adds that she is coming.
The second sentence asserts the sister, and accepting it removes the worlds where the speaker has none. Every update in (7) is an addition of asserted content to a common ground that already meets its requirements.
Stalnaker (1974) · Karttunen (1974) · (7) constructed.
Empirical

Accommodation

(8)My sister is coming to pick me up.
My sister presupposes that the speaker has a sister. If the hearer did not know it, the common ground does not yet contain it, and in the filled worlds the speaker has a sister.
Accommodation. The adjustment by which hearers add a missing presupposition to the background so that the utterance becomes acceptable, without friction or complaint. The hearer removes the worlds without a sister and then adds that she is coming.
Presupposition failure. Where everyone knows that the speaker has no sister, the common ground cannot be adjusted in this way, and the natural reply objects: You don't have a sister.
Lewis (1979) · von Fintel (2008) · (8) constructed.

Informative presupposition

(9)ADo you want to join us for lunch?BI have to pick up my sister from the airport.
My sister presupposes that B has a sister, and A, who has only just met B, cannot have known it. The exchange proceeds normally: A accepts that B has a sister and responds to the refusal.
Informative presupposition. A presupposition that is new information for the hearer, conveyed by being presented as already shared.
Stalnaker (1974), fn. 2, after Sadock · von Fintel (2008), Philosophical Perspectives 22 · (9) as given in Benbaji-Elhadad & Doron (2024).

How easily accommodation happens

(10), (12), (14) and (16) go through without a pause, while (11), (13) and (15) do not. Accommodation is easiest when the speaker is known to be knowledgeable and trustworthy, and the presupposed content is easy to integrate, uncontroversial and not what the conversation is disputing.
Lewis (1979) built a limit into accommodation: a missing presupposition comes into existence other things being equal and within certain limits. Content that is surprising or likely to be disputed, like the camel in (11) or the engagement in (15), resists accommodation.
What a hearer accommodates may be no more than the speaker's public commitment. After (13) the common ground can record that the speaker claims to own a giraffe without the hearer believing it.
Lewis (1979), Journal of Philosophical Logic 8.

Empirical

Too

(17)#Tonight, John is having dinner in New York, too.
(18)#I can't stand that linguist.
Too in (17) needs someone else already salient who is having dinner in New York tonight. Whether the conversation has mentioned such a person is a fact about the conversation itself, so no adjustment of beliefs can supply one.
The demonstrative in (18) fails for the same reason. That linguist needs a referent that is already salient, by mention or by pointing, and nothing the hearer could come to believe would make one salient.
The same sentence as (17) can open a novel without any oddness, because a reader joins a story already under way and reconstructs what has gone before.
Kripke (2009 [1990]), Linguistic Inquiry 40 · von Fintel (2008) · Tonhauser, Beaver, Roberts & Simons (2013) on demonstratives · (18) constructed.

Empirical

What accommodation is for

Thomason (1990). Hearers accommodate in order to remove obstacles to what they take the speaker to be trying to do.
In (19) presupposing the car saves stating it separately. In (20), a university notice, the factive regret presupposes the rule, and the regret is a courtesy.
In (21) the factive know presents the win as a fact, which overstates the evidence in order to project confidence.
(22) presupposes a transaction the reader never made, and a reader who accommodates it clicks through.
Thomason (1990), in Intentions in Communication · (19) Lorson, Cummins & Rohde (2019) · (20) Karttunen (1974) · (21), (22) a politician and a spam email.

Empirical

Asserting and presupposing

(23a) and (23b) both convey that the rent has gone up twice. (23a) asserts it, and (23b) asserts the latest increase and uses again to present the earlier one as known.
Information packaging. The choice between making content the main point of an utterance and making it background. Asserted content is offered for acceptance, and presupposed content is presented as accepted already.
As we've seen, these come with different objection conditions and strategies. Ordinary denials such as No, No, he didn't or I doubt it target what is asserted and leave the presupposition standing. An objection to what is presupposed has to interrupt first: Hey, wait a minute, he only put it up once.
(24b) is the natural choice when the cousin is only background, and (24a) sounds over-explicit because it makes the cousin part of what is asserted.
Shanon (1976), Foundations of Language 14 · von Fintel (2004) · (23), (24) constructed.
Empirical

Projection tests

(25)
Hypothesis Nnegation
If p is a presupposition of S, it is also a presupposition of the negation of S. (25a) Sam didn't quit smoking still takes it that Sam used to smoke.
Hypothesis Qquestions
If p is a presupposition of S, it is also a presupposition of the question version of S, as in (25b) Did Sam quit smoking?
Hypothesis Cconditionals
If p is a presupposition of S, it is also a presupposition of if S, then S′, as in (25c) If Sam quit smoking, his marathon time should improve.
Projection. The inheritance of a presupposition by a larger sentence that contains its trigger.
The projection problem. To predict, from the parts of a complex sentence and the way they are combined, which presuppositions the whole sentence carries.
Langendoen & Savin (1971) · Karttunen (1973).
Empirical

Holes and plugs

(26)aSam didn't quit smoking.bMaria says that Sam quit smoking.
Hole. An embedding construction that lets the presuppositions of what it embeds through to the whole sentence. Negation, questions, possibility modals and the antecedent of a conditional are holes, so (26a) still presupposes that Sam used to smoke.
Plug. An embedding construction that stops them. Verbs of saying such as say, tell and promise are plugs, so a speaker of (26b) is not committed to Sam's ever having smoked.
Karttunen (1973), Linguistic Inquiry 4 · (26) constructed.

Empirical

Filtering

(27) presupposes that Jack has children. (28) and (30) contain (27) but can be said by someone unsure whether Jack has any children, and (29) asserts that he does.
In each case the first part supplies what the second part needs: the antecedent of if, the first conjunct, or the falsity of the first disjunct.
In (31) the second conjunct comes too late to supply what the first needs, so the order of the parts matters and the sentence still takes Jack's children for granted.
Karttunen (1973), Linguistic Inquiry 4 · (31) after Karttunen (1974).
Empirical

Filtering and world knowledge

(32)Either Geraldine isn't a Mormon or she has given up wearing her holy underwear.
The second disjunct presupposes that Geraldine has worn holy underwear, and (32) does not presuppose it. Yet the negation of the first disjunct, that Geraldine is a Mormon, does not by itself entail it.
It does once the context supplies the fact that Mormons wear holy underwear. Filtering draws on what the context contains as well as on what the sentence says.
Karttunen (1973), Linguistic Inquiry 4.

Stalnaker, Lewis, Karttunen & Heim

Presupposition as a property of conversations.
Historical

Stalnaker

Robert Stalnaker
born 1940
Pragmatics (1970) and Presuppositions (1973). Stalnaker moved presupposition from sentences to speakers. A speaker presupposes p when they take p for granted and assume that their audience does the same.
A sentence presupposes p, in a derived sense, when it can only appropriately be used by a speaker who presupposes p. Strawson's truth-value gaps become one possible source of such requirements among others.
Pragmatic Presuppositions (1974) gives a formal picture of this view.
Stalnaker (1970), Synthese 22 · Stalnaker (1973), Journal of Philosophical Logic 2 · Stalnaker (1974), Semantics and Philosophy.
Historical

Common ground

Common Ground (2002). For Stalnaker the common ground is what the participants mutually accept for the purposes of the conversation. Acceptance can fall short of belief, as when people assume something for the sake of argument.
Context set. The worlds in which every proposition in the common ground is true, which are the live options for how things are as far as the conversation is concerned.
Assertion (1978). To assert p is to propose adding p to the common ground. If no one objects, the worlds where p is false drop out of the context set. Stalnaker adds that an assertion should rule out part of the context set and leave part of it standing.
Stalnaker (1978), Syntax and Semantics 9 · Stalnaker (2002), Linguistics and Philosophy 25.
Historical

Lewis

David Lewis
1941 – 2001
Scorekeeping in a Language Game (1979). A conversation keeps score as a baseball game does. The score at each stage depends on what has happened so far and settles which moves are correct next.
Conversational score also adjusts itself to make moves correct, which no baseball score does. If something said requires a component to be a certain way, the score tends to change so that it is.
The paper works through this pattern for presupposition, permissibility, definite descriptions, come and go, standards of precision, relative modality and performatives.
Lewis (1979), Journal of Philosophical Logic 8.
Historical

The rule of accommodation

When an utterance needs presupposition P to be acceptable and P is not yet presupposed, P comes to be presupposed at that moment, other things being equal and within certain limits.Lewis (1979), paraphrased
(34)France is hexagonal.
Lewis applies the same rule beyond presupposition. Austin's (34) is acceptable as a rough description and false by a geographer's standards. An utterance that needs higher standards of precision raises them at once, and lowering them again is harder.
Lewis (1979) · (34) Austin (1962), How to Do Things with Words.
Historical

Karttunen

Lauri Karttunen
1941 – 2022
Born in Finland, Karttunen studied Finnish at Helsinki and took his PhD at Indiana in 1969. He taught semantics at Texas before computational work at Xerox PARC and Stanford.
Presuppositions of Compound Sentences (1973). The first systematic statement of the projection problem: how the presuppositions of a complex sentence depend on those of its parts.
The notions of discourse referents and implicative verbs such as manage also come from his work.
Karttunen (1973), Linguistic Inquiry 4 · ACL obituary (2022) · Virittäjä 4/2022.
Historical

Holes, plugs and filters

(35)aThe prime minister of Switzerland is not visiting Dublin.bMaria says that the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.cIf Switzerland has a prime minister, the prime minister of Switzerland is visiting Dublin.
Negation in (35a) lets through the presupposition that Switzerland has a prime minister, says in (35b) keeps it from becoming the speaker's, and the antecedent in (35c) supplies it.
The cumulative hypothesis. Every presupposition of every part of a sentence must project. Filters are particularly problematic counterexamples to this hypothesis.
Karttunen (1973) · Langendoen & Savin (1971).
Historical

Presupposition and Linguistic Context (1974). Karttunen recast filtering in terms of contexts. Each part of a sentence is checked against its own local context, which is the initial context plus whatever the earlier parts contribute.

Historical

Heim

Irene Heim
born 1954
On the Projection Problem for Presuppositions (1983). Heim takes the meaning of a sentence to be its context change potential, a function from contexts to contexts.
A presupposition is a condition on when that function is defined. The projection behaviour of not, and and if follows from how each connective builds its context change potential from those of its parts.
Local accommodation. Heim's term for adding a missing presupposition inside an embedded update when adding it to the global context is impossible.
Heim (1983), WCCFL 2.

Context change

Presupposition as a condition on updating a context.
Formal

Context sets

\[ c + S \;=\; \{\, w \in c : \sem{S}(w) = 1 \,\} \]
(36)Jack has children.The filled worlds are those where Jack has children.
A context is modelled by its context set c. Asserting S, if the assertion is accepted, removes every world in which S is false.
Stalnaker (1978) · Heim (1983).
Formal

Update and definedness

\[ c + S \text{ is defined only if } c \subseteq p, \quad\text{where } \sem{S} \text{ is defined only if } p \]
A presupposition of S is a condition on the input context: every world in c must be a p-world before S can update it. This is the definedness condition on \(\sem{S}\) applied to contexts.
(37)Alex stopped smoking.\(c + (37)\) is defined only if Alex used to smoke in every world in \(c\). Where defined, \(c + (37) = \{\, w \in c : \text{Alex does not smoke now in } w \,\}\).
Stalnaker's bridge. von Fintel's name for the principle that a sentence can update a body of information only if it has a truth value in every world of that body.
Heim (1983) · Heim & Kratzer (1998), ch. 4 · von Fintel (2008).
Formal

When the presupposition is not met

\[ c + (37) \text{ requires } \forall w \in c\,[\text{Alex used to smoke in } w] \]
Suppose c leaves it open whether Alex used to smoke. The filled worlds are those where he did, and the condition fails at the others, so \(c + (37)\) is undefined.
An undefined update is an instruction that cannot be carried out until the context has been repaired, and that repair is accommodation.
Presupposition and assertion act on the same context in different ways. The presupposition is a condition on the update, and the assertion is what the update adds.
Heim (1983) · Beaver (2001).
Formal

Accommodation as update

When c does not guarantee p, a hearer who accommodates first removes the worlds where p is false and then performs the update. For (9) the first step is \(c \cap \{\, w : \text{B has a sister in } w \,\}\).
The context that has to satisfy the presupposition is the one changed by the utterance itself, before its content is accepted. Stalnaker compares I, which needs a speaker that only the act of speaking provides.
Stalnaker (1998) · von Fintel (2008).
Formal

Connectives

\[ \begin{aligned} c + \text{not } A &\;=\; c \setminus (c + A) \\ c + (A \text{ and } B) &\;=\; (c + A) + B \\ c + (\text{if } A, B) &\;=\; c \setminus \bigl((c + A) \setminus ((c + A) + B)\bigr) \\ c + (A \text{ or } B) &\;=\; (c + A) \cup \bigl((c \setminus (c + A)) + B\bigr) \end{aligned} \]
Negation first updates c with A and then removes the result, so c must admit A. The presupposition passes through, which is why not is a hole.
Conjunction updates c with A and then updates the result with B, so the presupposition of B only has to hold in \(c + A\). The consequent of a conditional is likewise evaluated in \(c + A\), and the second disjunct is evaluated in the worlds where the first is false.
Heim (1983) · disjunction after Karttunen (1974) and Beaver (2001).

Formal

Filtering by hand

Filled worlds are those where Jack has children (A), and the darker ones those where all of them are bald (B). In (29) the first update removes the empty worlds, so the second update is defined whatever c was. In (31) the first update is the one that needs every world to be filled, and nothing has removed the empty worlds from c beforehand.
In (30) the second disjunct updates \(c \setminus (c + {}\)Jack has no children\()\), the worlds where Jack has children, so it too is always defined.
Karttunen (1973) · Heim (1983).
Formal

Local accommodation

(38)There is no King of France. So the King of France isn't hiding in this room.
\[ c \setminus \bigl((c \cap K) + A\bigr) \;=\; c \setminus \varnothing \;=\; c \]
After the first sentence the context entails that there is no king, so global accommodation is impossible. Heim lets the hearer add the presupposition inside the negation, where K is the proposition that France has a king.
Since \(c \cap K\) is empty, the inner update is empty and the negation leaves c unchanged. The second sentence adds nothing to the first, which is what so signals.
Heim ranks global accommodation first and local as a last resort. For von Fintel (2008) the local case is a grammatical adjustment of the sentence and a different process from accommodation proper.
Heim (1983) · (38) after von Fintel (2008).
Formal

The proviso problem

(39)If Theo hates sonnets, so does his wife.
(40)If John is a scuba diver, he'll bring his wetsuit.
The conditional clause predicts that (39) presupposes only that if Theo hates sonnets he has a wife. Hearers take it to presuppose that Theo has a wife. Geurts called this gap the proviso problem.
von Fintel (2008). The semantics delivers the conditional presupposition, and accommodation delivers the stronger one. A speaker of (39) is far more likely to know that Theo has a wife than to know only the conditional.
In (40) the conditional is what hearers take away, so a theory that always predicted the unconditional presupposition would fail here.
Geurts (1996), Linguistics and Philosophy 19 · von Fintel (2008) · (40) as usually cited.
Formal

The explanatory problem

\[ c + (A \text{ and}^{*} B) \;=\; (c + B) + A \]
Heim's clauses describe projection without explaining it. A connective and* defined as above has the same truth conditions as and but would let the second conjunct filter the first, and nothing in the framework rules it out.
Schlenker (2009). The local context of an expression is the strongest restriction that can be placed on it without changing what the sentence says, given the context and the words before it. For the second conjunct of A and B this is c plus A, so Karttunen's result is derived from the truth conditions.
Rooth, in correspondence reported in Heim (1990) · Soames (1989) · Schlenker (2008), Theoretical Linguistics 34 · Schlenker (2009), Semantics & Pragmatics 2.

Two diagnostics

(41) attributes to Kim the belief that Alex used to smoke. (42) does not attribute to Kim the belief that Ruth is a doctor.
Obligatory local effect. Content has this property when, embedded under an operator such as believe, it must be part of the operator's scope, as in (41).
Strong contextual felicity. A trigger has this property when it is acceptable only in a context that already entails its projective content, as too is in (17).
Tonhauser, Beaver, Roberts & Simons (2013), Language 89 · (41), (42) constructed.

Applied

Answering a loaded question

Every answer to a question shares the question's presupposition. Yes and no to (43) both take the embezzling for granted, so answering at all accommodates it. In an American courtroom a question of this kind draws the objection that it assumes facts not in evidence.
A hearer can reject the presupposition before answering, or instead of answering: Hey, wait a minute, I have never taken money from the charity. A metalinguistic No, I haven't stopped, I never started rejects it too, though a listener who catches only the first words hears an admission.
For (44) and (45), a corrective answer denies that any answer applies. Kaplan built a database interface that gave such answers, because a bare None to (45) is literally true and misleading.
Kaplan (1978), Theoretical Issues in Natural Language Processing 2 · Kaplan (1982), Artificial Intelligence 19 · (43), (44) constructed.
Applied

Evasion

Clayman (2001). In broadcast news interviews answering remains the norm, so an interviewee who resists a question's agenda is accountable for doing so and, in Clayman's phrase, engages in damage control.
Overt resistance is announced: the interviewee objects to the question or its premise and softens the move, for instance by deferring to the interviewer or giving a reason.
Covert resistance goes unmarked. In operating on the question, the interviewee restates the question in a more answerable form, often without its presupposition, and answers that version.
Clayman (2001), Language in Society 30 · Clayman & Heritage (2002), The News Interview, ch. 7.

Framing

(46a) and (46b) convey the same accusation, packaged as (23a) and (23b) package the rent increase. In (46b) the change-of-state verb presents the manipulation as settled, so the accusation is made without being offered for discussion.
Packaging is one kind of framing. A speaker with a proposition to convey chooses among the sentences that express it, and the choice decides how easy the hearer's task is, what the maxims add, and whose perspective the issue is seen from.
Lakoff (2004). Negating a frame evokes it, so I am not a crook keeps crookedness in view, much as Hypothesis N keeps a presupposition in place under negation.

Lorson, Cummins & Rohde (2019). Fifty participants in Edinburgh played a detective questioning a suspect, with instructions to challenge every lie. Eight answers contained a falsehood, asserted in one version of the script and presupposed in the other.
False presupposed content was challenged less often. When participants did object, they used no more words and no more hesitations, and they objected directly without needing a Hey, wait a minute. Domaneschi et al. (2018). In an ERP study, presuppositions that had to be accommodated produced an N400 followed by a P600. With definite descriptions the N400 was more prominent, reflecting the search for an antecedent. With change-of-state verbs the P600 was more prominent, reflecting the later updating of the discourse model.
Masia et al. (2023). In tweets by Italian politicians, content presupposed by definite descriptions was processed more shallowly than content presupposed by change-of-state verbs.
Lombardi Vallauri et al. (2025) relate the N400 effects to a mismatch between how content is packaged and its status in the discourse, and find no link with the hearer's epistemic vigilance.

Readback

(49)We are now at take-off.KLM 4805, Tenerife, 27 March 1977.
PH-BUF, Rijn
Heathrow, 1975
At Tenerife the KLM crew read back a route clearance and ended with (49). The captain began the take-off roll without a take-off clearance, and the collision with a Pan Am 747 still on the runway killed 583 people.
ICAO phraseology now reserves take-off for the clearance itself and its cancellation. At every other point pilots and controllers say departure.
The pilot reads back the safety-related parts of every clearance and the controller checks the readback, so nothing safety-critical is left for a hearer to accommodate.

Applied

Clinical communication

Lingard et al. (2004). Over 90 hours and 48 procedures in a Canadian teaching hospital, observers recorded 421 communication events, and 129 of them (about 30%) failed. A third of the failures had visible effects, from delay to procedural error.
The failed exchanges came too late, were incomplete or inaccurate, left the issue unresolved, or left out someone who needed to know. Many of them left the hearer to reconstruct background the speaker never stated.
Lingard et al. (2008). A short checklist briefing before 302 procedures cut communication failures from 3.95 to 1.31 per procedure.
Between clinicians a compressed register and shared training make accommodation cheap and usually safe. With a patient the common ground is much thinner, and King and Hoppe's (2013) review of patient-centred communication recommends plain language, explicit explanation and teach-back, in which the patient restates the plan in their own words.
Lingard et al. (2004), Quality & Safety in Health Care 13 · Lingard et al. (2008), Archives of Surgery 143 · King & Hoppe (2013), Journal of Graduate Medical Education 5.
